# Optional JA-MENTOR instrument norms

Scope: non-core modules only (CATS, a 23-item mental-health literacy block, nine-item social-media and gaming problem sets, DASC, C-SSRS, MoBa sleep). Core instruments (BCFPI, ACE-IQ, KIDSCREEN-10, CYRM-12) were not reviewed. Youth and adolescent figures are preferred, European where a source exists. Date of search: 29 October 2026. No number below was estimated or copied from a different scale.

## What cutoff and means apply to the CATS youth self-report?

### Takeaway
The exposure list and the 20 past-two-week symptom items scored 0–3 (total 0–60) are the youth self-report of the Child and Adolescent Trauma Screen (CATS; Sachser and colleagues). The published youth event checklist has 15 yes/no events, not 16. The score the instrument actually uses for probable PTSD is that 0–60 DSM-5 symptom total. The consortium’s original recommendation is ≥21, with 15–20 as moderate distress. A 2022 interview validation of the same DSM-5 total (CATS-2) keeps ≥21 as a sensitive screen and adds ≥25 when higher specificity is wanted. A trauma-clinic self-report mean is about 24. No unselected school or community mean was found.

### Cited Findings
- The published youth self-report event list has 15 yes/no events: serious natural disaster; serious accident or injury; threatened, hit, or hurt badly within the family; the same in school or the community; attacked, stabbed, shot at, or robbed; seeing someone in the family hurt; seeing someone in school or the community hurt; sexual abuse; online or social-media sexual pressure; in-person bullying; online bullying; sudden or violent death of someone close; stressful or scary medical procedure; being around war; other. Symptom items 1–20 cover the past two weeks and are scored 0 never, 1 once in a while, 2 half the time, 3 almost always. The total is the sum of those 20 items, range 0–60. The form cites Sachser et al. (2017). — [APA CATS youth form](https://www.apaservices.org/practice/reimbursement/health-registry/child-adolescent-trauma-screen.pdf)
- For ages 7–17, the 2017 scoring sheet bands the 0–60 total as below 15 (not clinically elevated), 15–20 (moderate trauma-related distress), and 21 or higher (probable PTSD). The preschool caregiver form uses different bands and is not the metric here. — [CATS scoring tip sheet, 2017](https://uwhatc.uw.edu/PDF/TF-%20CBT/pages/3%20Assessment/Standardized%20Measures/CATS-Scoring-Tip-Sheet-2017.pdf)
- The ISTSS CATS page states that preliminary cutoffs were estimated from an earlier DSM-IV questionnaire, and recommends a total of ≥21 on items 1–20 (range 0–60) as a clinically relevant level for ages 7–17. — [ISTSS CATS page](https://istss.org/clinical-resources/child-adolescent-trauma-assessments/child-and-adolescent-trauma-screen/)
- The 2017 psychometric paper (US, German, and Norwegian trauma-exposed samples; self-report n = 249, 117, and 109) found Cronbach’s α of .88–.94 for the 20-item score and supported the four DSM-5 clusters. It states that external validation against a DSM-5 interview, and therefore determination of cut-points, was still pending. — [Sachser et al. 2017, Journal of Affective Disorders, DOI 10.1016/j.jad.2016.12.040](https://edoc.ku.de/id/eprint/19151/)
- CATS-2 (Sachser et al. 2022) re-validated the DSM-5 20-item self-report total against the CAPS-CA-5 in 121 interviewed children and adolescents (AUC = .88). A score ≥21 was the sensitive screening cutoff for probable PTSD; ≥25 had higher specificity for a PTSD diagnosis. Those thresholds are for the DSM-5 0–60 total. Separate ICD-11 cutoffs (≥7 sensitive, ≥9 more specific) apply to the shorter ICD-11 PTSD item set, not to the 0–60 total. The CPTSD cutoff was not validated. — [Sachser et al. 2022, European Journal of Psychotraumatology, DOI 10.1080/20008066.2022.2105580](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9344962/)
- In that CATS-2 clinical sample (N = 283 self-reports, ages 7–17, mean age 12.21, SD 3.16, 59% female; trauma services in the United States, Norway, and Germany), the DSM-5 PTSD self-report mean was 24.16 (SD 12.26). Country means were United States 22.70 (SD 12.57, n = 95), Norway 22.36 (SD 11.66, n = 100), and Germany 27.80 (SD 11.91, n = 88). Mean number of traumatic events on self-report was 3.84 (SD 2.20). Living situation included foster or adoptive parents for 51.1% of the US subsample and child welfare for 10.2% of the German subsample. Self-report DSM-5 α = .89. — [Sachser et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9344962/)

### Inferences
- The dictionary’s 16 exposure items are one more than the published 15-item youth checklist, but every event type named in the assignment appears on that checklist. The 20 symptom items and the 0–60 total identify the youth self-report, not the caregiver form and not the ICD-11-only score.
- For a benchmark on the 0–60 total, ≥21 is the threshold the original materials recommend, and ≥25 is the later, interview-based, higher-specificity revision of that same total. The 15–20 band is moderate distress, not probable PTSD.
- The clinical mean of 24 sits between those two thresholds. It is a trauma-service mean, including some child-welfare placements, not a community mean and not a refugee-clinic mean.

### Gaps
- No unselected community or school mean for the 0–60 youth self-report was found.
- No refugee-sample mean was found.
- The 2017 paper does not report symptom means, and it did not empirically set the ≥21 cutoff. Means above are from the 2022 clinical CATS-2 sample.
- Why the dictionary counts 16 exposure items rather than 15 was not resolved (a local extra item, or a write-in counted as its own item, are both possible). Item text for a 16th event was not available.

## Which mental-health literacy questionnaire is the 23-item “how easy” block, and what norms does it have?

### Takeaway
The stems match the German MHLS-GER-Q24 (Mental Health Literacy Survey–Germany–Questionnaire 24; Orkan Okan and colleagues), an HLS-EU-style scale of perceived ease, not the O’Connor Mental Health Literacy Scale. The published form has 24 items, not 23. Higher scores mean information feels easier to manage. Published figures are for German adults and apprentices, not school-age adolescents. Numeric boundaries for the four proficiency levels were not in the sources read.

### Cited Findings
- MHLS-GER-Q24 adapts the European Health Literacy Survey Questionnaire to mental health. The stem is “Wie einfach oder schwierig ist es für Sie, …” (How easy or difficult is it for you …). The 24 items include: keeping everyday mental strain and stress from getting the upper hand; judging whether information on services is free of commercial interest; judging the accuracy of mental-health information; judging whether professional help is needed; contacting a professional service for one’s own mental illness; understanding information on how to cope with difficult situations; judging whether media information on mental illness is trustworthy; understanding media reports about mental illness; and understanding what a GP, psychotherapist, or psychiatrist says. Responses run from “sehr schwierig” (very difficult) to “sehr einfach” (very easy), plus “weiß nicht” (don’t know). — [Wort & Bild Verlag, Impact, December 2024 (instrument text and study description)](https://www.wortundbildverlag.de/sites/wortundbildverlag.de/files/2024-12/AU_Impact_12-2024.pdf)
- A conference abstract on the same 24-item tool reports Cronbach’s α = .96 in n = 2,007 German internet users. The mean item score was 2.42, where 1 = “very difficult” and 4 = “very easy”. Across responses, 54.3% were “very difficult” or “rather difficult”. The presumed 4-competence and 3-domain factor models did not fit; one factor explained 49% of variance. — [European Journal of Public Health abstract, DOI 10.1093/eurpub/ckaf161.1928](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurpub/ckaf161.1928)
- The public report of the Okan and Kolpatzik study (about 2,000 adults aged 18 and over, plus 500 apprentices, surveyed online in July–August 2024) classifies results into four levels named inadequate, problematic, sufficient, and excellent. It states that 86% of the general-population sample had low mental-health literacy. Among apprentices, 79.6% were low and 20.4% high; 46.6% of apprentices were in the inadequate level. The report describes apprentices as young people in training. The representative adult sample was recruited from age 18. — [Impact, December 2024](https://www.wortundbildverlag.de/sites/wortundbildverlag.de/files/2024-12/AU_Impact_12-2024.pdf)
- The Technical University of Munich Health Literacy Unit describes this questionnaire as the mental-health adaptation of the European health-literacy survey questionnaire. — [TUM Health Literacy Unit, MHLS-Germany](https://www.hs.mh.tum.de/healthliteracy/forschung/mhls-germany/)
- A different German instrument, MHLS-GER, is a translation of O’Connor and Casey’s Mental Health Literacy Scale. It covers knowledge and attitudes, uses mixed 4- and 5-point items, and in the German evaluation has 31 items rather than the original 35. The original sum runs from 35 to 160, with higher scores meaning more literacy. Those items and that range do not match the “how easy is it” stems. — [Fischer et al., Journal of Psychosomatic Research, German MHLS](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022395624004576)

### Inferences
- The dictionary block is MHLS-GER-Q24, or a 23-item subset of it. Item 8 of the published form is about workplace offers to improve wellbeing. A youth questionnaire could drop that item and land on 23. That is a plausible account of the count mismatch, not something the sources confirm.
- Direction is higher = easier = higher literacy. The metric in the peer-reviewed abstract is the 1–4 item mean, not a 0–60-style symptom total.
- The 86% and 79.6% figures are adult and apprentice population shares in Germany. They are not school-age norms, and they should not be applied unchanged if the survey dropped an item.
- Norms from the O’Connor MHLS (or its 31-item German knowledge-and-attitude version) do not belong on this block.

### Gaps
- No child or school-adolescent mean, and no proficiency distribution for under-18s, was found. Apprentice results are the youngest group reported, and the representative sample was aged 18 and over.
- The magazine names the four HLS-style levels but the sources read do not state the numeric index cut-points that separate inadequate, problematic, sufficient, and excellent.
- It is not confirmed which of the 24 items is absent from the 23-item dictionary block.
- The conference abstract (n = 2,007; mean 2.42; 54.3% of responses difficult) and the magazine (about 2,000 adults plus 500 apprentices; 86% of people low) are related reports. They use different metrics (share of item responses versus share of people). They were not treated as one sample.

## What are the nine-item German social-media and gaming scales, and what are the published cutoffs and adolescent means?

### Takeaway
The nine agreement items are the symptom items of two separate ICD-11 scales from Paschke, Austermann, and Thomasius: SOMEDIS-A for social media and GADIS-A for gaming. The published response format is the one the survey uses: strongly disagree 0 to strongly agree 4, nine-item sum 0–36, last 12 months. Each published scale also has a tenth time-criterion item that the dictionary’s 1–9 blocks do not include. Disorder is not a single 0–36 cutoff. In German adolescents aged 10–17 who use the medium at least weekly, about 3–4% met the full published rule. Means below are for the two subscales, reported as standard errors, and are split by that classification. A full-sample mean and SD of the 0–36 sum was not given.

### Cited Findings
- GADIS-A has nine symptom statements plus one frequency item. For the last 12 months, items 1–9 are scored strongly disagree 0, somewhat disagree 1, partially agree/partially disagree 2, somewhat agree 3, strongly agree 4. The nine-item sum has a maximum of 36. Item 10 asks how often problems occurred in the past year: 0 not at all, 1 only on single days, 2 for longer periods, 3 nearly daily. The time criterion is met at 2 or higher. The nine statements are: play more often and longer than planned; cannot stop; give up interests outside gaming; neglect daily duties; continue despite conflict with others; continue although school, apprenticeship, or work performance is harmed; neglect appearance, hygiene, or health; risk or lose important relationships; disadvantages at school, apprenticeship, or work. — [Paschke, Austermann, and Thomasius 2020, Journal of Clinical Medicine, DOI 10.3390/jcm9040993](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7230491/)
- On GADIS-A, negative consequences (factor 1) are items 3, 6, 7, 8, and 9. Cognitive-behavioral symptoms (factor 2) are items 1, 2, 4, and 5. Youden cutoffs against ICD-11-related Internet Gaming Disorder Scale items were 12.5 for the 0–36 total (specificity 83.12%, sensitivity 97.96%, AUC 95.1%), 5.5 for factor 1, and 9.5 for factor 2. The rule the authors then applied was factor 1 > 5, factor 2 > 9, and total ≥ 12, plus the time criterion. That rule classified 3.7% (95% CI 2.4–5.0; table: 3.66%, 2.38–4.95; N = 30 of 819) of frequent gamers as gaming disorder. — [Paschke et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7230491/)
- Those 819 adolescents were frequent gamers aged 10–17 (at least weekly). In the wider weekly-gamer group (N = 1,014), mean age was 13.01 (SD 2.36) and 40.1% were girls. Among adolescents classified as not disordered (n = 789), mean factor 1 was 2.13 (SE 0.11) and mean factor 2 was 4.94 (SE 0.13). Among those classified as disordered (n = 30), means were 13.30 (SE 0.78) and 13.53 (SE 0.39). — [Paschke et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7230491/)
- SOMEDIS-A is the same nine statements and the same 0–4 format, rewritten for social media, plus the same tenth time item (criterion met at 2 or above). Social media was defined as services on which texts, photos, animations, or videos can be shared, commented on, or liked. The nine-item maximum is 36. — [Paschke, Austermann, and Thomasius 2021, Frontiers in Psychiatry, DOI 10.3389/fpsyt.2021.661483](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8100192/)
- SOMEDIS-A factor cutoffs are not the same as GADIS-A. Youden values were 6.5 for factor 1, negative consequences (specificity 87.39%, sensitivity 81.48%, AUC 88.4%), and 8.5 for factor 2, cognitive-behavioral symptoms (specificity 82.96%, sensitivity 88.89%, AUC 88.8%). The authors classified social-media use disorder when factor 1 was > 6 and factor 2 was > 8 and the time criterion was met. They did not publish a single 0–36 cutoff as the rule they applied. Prevalence was 3.3% (table: 3.33%, 95% CI 2.18–4.48; N = 31 of 931). — [Paschke et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8100192/)
- The SOMEDIS-A sample was a representative German parent–adolescent sample, final N = 931 adolescents who used social media at least weekly, ages 10–17, mean age 13.67 (SD 2.19). Adolescents without the disorder (n = 900) had mean factor 1 of 2.43 (SE 0.10) and mean factor 2 of 4.81 (SE 0.12). Those with the disorder (n = 31) had 13.13 (SE 0.88) and 12.45 (SE 0.49). A latent-profile “problematic” group, which is not the same as the cutoff rule, was 6.34% (95% CI 4.77–7.9). Fieldwork for this social-media paper was during the COVID-19 pandemic. — [Paschke et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8100192/)
- The nine-item yes/no Social Media Disorder Scale (van den Eijnden and colleagues) and the nine-item yes/no Internet Gaming Disorder Scale are different instruments. Both papers used those yes/no scales only as validity criteria. Their sums are counts of “yes” answers from 0 to 9, not 0–36 agreement scores. — [Paschke et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7230491/); [Paschke et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8100192/)

### Inferences
- smadd1–9 is SOMEDIS-A items 1–9. gadd1–9 is GADIS-A items 1–9. The survey’s 0–4 agreement coding matches the published scoring. The scales should stay separate: the factor cutoffs differ.
- A 0–36 sum can be described, but the published disorder classification also requires the two factor thresholds and item 10. If the survey did not field item 10, the 3.3% and 3.7% rates are not directly reproducible from the nine items alone.
- The GADIS-A total threshold used in classification was ≥12, while the Youden point was 12.5. For integer scores, 12.5 would mean 13 or higher. Both figures are in the paper; the prevalence uses ≥12 together with the factor and time rules.
- These means are among weekly users, not among all adolescents, and the paper reports standard errors for the subgroup means, not a full-sample mean and SD.

### Gaps
- No full-sample mean and SD of the 0–36 sum was located for either scale.
- No clinical-treatment sample mean (inpatient or day-clinic) was reported; patients were used only to check whether the GADIS-A wording was understandable.
- Item 10’s presence or absence in the JA-MENTOR export was not verified beyond the assignment’s statement that the blocks are nine items each.

## What is the published DASC, and which benchmarks apply when the items are not in the export?

### Takeaway
The section name matches the Digital Addiction Scale for Children (DASC; Hawi, Samaha, and Griffiths, 2019), including Spanish-language use of that scale. It is 25 items, scored 1 never to 5 always, total 25–125, higher meaning more digital addiction. No published diagnostic cutoff was found. The development and adaptation samples are late childhood and early adolescence (about 9–14 years), and a sample total mean was not in the sources read. The survey export does not show the items, so the match is by the section name only.

### Cited Findings
- The DASC is a 25-item self-report for children aged 9–12, covering video gaming, social media, and texting. Development sample: 822 participants, 54.2% male, grades 4–7. Internal consistency α = 0.936. The abstract does not report a cutoff or a mean total. — [Hawi, Samaha, and Griffiths 2019, Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking, DOI 10.1089/cyber.2019.0132](https://doi.org/10.1089/cyber.2019.0132)
- Later descriptions of that scale: each item is 1 (never) to 5 (always); the total is the sum of all 25 items, range 25–125; higher scores mean higher digital addiction. Two factors are used: an interpersonal set of 13 items and an intrapersonal set of 12. — [Turkish psychometric paper, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10122085/); [Spanish school study, DOI 10.5964/ejop.17937](https://doi.org/10.5964/ejop.17937)
- A Spanish translation was completed by 977 school pupils in Castilla-La Mancha and Murcia, ages 9–14 (mean 10.65, SD 1.42); 84.5% were aged 9–11 and 15.5% (n = 151) were 12–14. The paper calls the instrument the Spanish DASC. It does not, in the text retrieved, give a raw total mean or a cutoff. Girls had lower latent interpersonal and intrapersonal means than boys (differences −0.197 and −0.261). Those are model-based differences, not raw score means. — [DOI 10.5964/ejop.17937](https://doi.org/10.5964/ejop.17937)
- A Turkish school sample of the DASC had 670 pupils aged 9–14 (mean 11.34, SD 1.61). The paper reports item means, not a cutoff. The highest item mean was 3.18 (“When I am not at school, I spend a lot of time using my device”). The total correlated r = 0.72 with a video-game addiction scale. — [PMC10122085](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10122085/)

### Inferences
- “Escala Digital de Adicción para Niños” is the Spanish name of this Hawi scale, not a different instrument with its own norms. Because the export has one collapsed variable and no item text, it is not possible to check that all 25 items, the 1–5 metric, or the two-factor scoring were retained.
- The scale is a severity sum. Sources reviewed do not support a probable-addiction cutoff comparable to the GADIS-A or CATS thresholds.
- Available samples stop at about age 14 and are mostly under 12. They are weak benchmarks for mid-adolescents.

### Gaps
- No diagnostic or screening cutoff was found in the development abstract or in the Spanish and Turkish adaptations reviewed.
- No sample mean and SD of the 25–125 total was found in those sources.
- Item content of the JA-MENTOR DASC section could not be checked.
- A paper whose title is exactly “Escala Digital de Adicción para Niños”, as opposed to a Spanish translation of Hawi’s DASC, was not found.

## What counts as a positive C-SSRS screen for ideation and for behaviour, and how common is a positive screen in adolescents?

### Takeaway
The recent C-SSRS screener is a short yes/no gate, not a symptom total, and it has no normal score. In the school form, past-month wish to be dead and past-month suicidal thoughts are the ideation questions; method, intent, and plan are asked only if thoughts are present. Suicidal behaviour is a separate lifetime question, with a past-three-month probe. Any yes leads at least to a behavioural-health referral; intent, a plan, or behaviour in the past three months is the higher-acuity result. No large European community prevalence for this recent screener was found.

### Cited Findings
- The Columbia school screener (past month) asks everyone: (1) “Have you wished you were dead or wished you could go to sleep and not wake up?” and (2) “Have you actually had any thoughts of killing yourself?” If the answer to 2 is yes, it asks (3) whether the person has been thinking about how they might do this, (4) whether there was some intention of acting on the thoughts, and (5) whether they have worked out the details and intend to carry out the plan. Question 6 is asked even when question 2 is no: “Have you ever done anything, started to do anything, or prepared to do anything to end your life?” If yes, it asks whether that was within the past three months. Behaviour examples include attempts and preparatory acts. — [C-SSRS Screener with Triage Points for Schools, 2026](https://cssrs.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/C-SSRS-Screener-with-Triage-Points-for-Schools-2026.pdf)
- On that school form, a yes to item 1, item 2, item 3, or lifetime item 6 is triaged to a behavioural-health referral. A yes to item 4, item 5, or item 6 within the past three months is triaged to student safety precautions and psychiatric evaluation (crisis team, emergency medical services, or emergency room). — [C-SSRS school screener, 2026](https://cssrs.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/C-SSRS-Screener-with-Triage-Points-for-Schools-2026.pdf)
- The screener is six yes/no questions. Ideation questions 3–5 are skipped when question 2 is no. If behaviour is endorsed, the interviewer asks whether it was within the last three months. There is a past-month version and other time frames; the scale is not scored as a symptom total. — [Suicidality among youth in the legal system, PMC12530505](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12530505/)
- In 284 probation-referred youth in one US Midwest county (2020–2023), past-month endorsement was 24.7% wish to be dead, 21.5% suicidal ideation, 7.8% method, 10.2% intent, and 3.2% intent with a plan. Suicidal behaviour within the past three months was 2.1%. Lifetime behaviour was 17.3%. This is a legal-system sample, not a community sample. — [PMC12530505](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12530505/)
- A convenience sample of 236 senior high school students aged 18 and over in one public school in Metro Manila, using the C-SSRS, reported suicidal ideation in 36.44% and suicidal behaviour in 19.06%. The abstract does not state that this was the six-item recent screener, and the time frame is not clearly the past month. — [Reyes, WASET abstract](https://publications.waset.org/abstracts/211957/a-cross-sectional-study-on-the-prevalence-and-severity-of-suicidal-ideations-and-behaviors-of-senior-high-school-students-using-the-columbia-suicide-severity-rating-scale-c-ssrs)
- A systematic review of pediatric emergency-department screening reported that, where a C-SSRS version was applied to all patients, about one-fifth screened positive, with Hill et al. at 19%. Among psychiatric emergency patients the positive range was 66% to 86%. These are emergency-department figures, not community figures. — [Scudder et al. 2022, Frontiers in Psychiatry](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.916731/full)

### Inferences
- Ideation on the usual recent screener is a past-month yes to wish to be dead (question 1) and/or thoughts of killing oneself (question 2). Method, intent, and plan describe the severity of those thoughts; they are not a separate total.
- Behaviour is a yes to question 6. Lifetime behaviour and behaviour in the past three months are different results. The school form treats past-three-month behaviour, intent, and a plan as the higher-acuity screen.
- A summed “normal score” does not exist for this screener. Item yes/no answers, and the triage those answers trigger, are the metric.

### Gaps
- No large European, or other unselected community, prevalence of a positive recent six-item screener was found. The probation figures are elevated by design. The Manila school figure is one convenience sample of students already 18 or older, and it may be the full C-SSRS rather than the recent screener.
- Studies do not all use the same definition of a “positive screen” (any yes, versus intent/plan/recent behaviour only). The school triage above is the Columbia form’s rule, not a universal research cutoff.

## Do the MoBa sleep items form a named scale with published norms?

### Takeaway
No. The items labelled as coming from the Norwegian Mother, Father and Child Cohort (MoBa) questionnaire are sleep questions without a published total. A named scale such as the Insomnia Severity Index, or a MoBa sleep score with norms, could not be identified from the materials that match a block of about 20 items. No norms are attached.

### Cited Findings
- MoBa’s 18-year questionnaire documents a short sleep block (difficulty falling asleep, repeated night waking, daytime tiredness or sleepiness, how long the problems have lasted, and weekday sleep duration). The documentation says these questions were adapted from the Karolinska Sleep Questionnaire, the Bergen Insomnia Scale, and a sleep diary. It does not define a summed MoBa sleep score. — [MoBa 18-year instrument documentation, Norwegian Institute of Public Health](https://www.fhi.no/contentassets/1016188d845f4c5f8fa57266c454ad8c/instrument-documentation-18-year_.pdf)
- A MoBa youth form (MoBaUng questionnaire 6) asks about current sleep with branching follow-ups: trouble falling asleep, waking at night, waking too early, daytime sleepiness, daytime tiredness, how many times a week, and how long the problem has lasted. That is a questionnaire module, not a named scale with a total. — [MoBaUng skjema 6, version C, 10 September 2020](https://www.fhi.no/globalassets/dokumenterfiler/studier/den-norske-mor-far-og-barn--undersokelsenmoba/sporreskjemaer/mobaung-skjema-6---versjon-c---20200910.pdf)
- The Bergen Insomnia Scale is six items, each 0–7 days, and can be summed. That item count does not match a block of about 20 items, and the MoBa documentation describes adaptation of questions rather than administration of the full scale. — [Bergen Insomnia Scale form, Helse Bergen](https://www.helse-bergen.no/4a3ebb/siteassets/seksjon/sovno/documents/bergen_insomnia_scale_nyekrit.pdf); [MoBa 18-year instrument documentation](https://www.fhi.no/contentassets/1016188d845f4c5f8fa57266c454ad8c/instrument-documentation-18-year_.pdf)

### Inferences
- Without the item text, these cannot be scored as the Insomnia Severity Index or the Bergen Insomnia Scale. The honest benchmark is that no published total applies.

### Gaps
- The dictionary does not contain the item text, so a one-to-one check against MoBaUng questionnaire 6 or the 18-year sleep questions was not possible.
- No MoBa-specific sleep total, cutoff, or adolescent norm was found.
