# ACE-IQ, KIDSCREEN-10, and CYRM-12 norms for the JA-MENTOR youth survey

Research date: 29 October 2026. The survey scoring described in the assignment is treated as authoritative. A published figure is translated onto that metric only when the items, anchors, reversals, and category rules match. Where a translation needs an assumption, the assumption is stated. Where it does not hold, the figure is left untranslated.

Sources were read as full texts or publisher PDFs where the fetch returned them. Snippets were not used as the sole basis for a number.

# ACE-IQ scoring and whether published totals sit on the survey metric

## What the survey scores, and what WHO scores

### Takeaway
The survey's two totals are the WHO 13-category binary score and the WHO 13-category frequency score, each 0 to 13. The category list and the frequency thresholds match the WHO analysis guidance. The one place the guidance is ambiguous, and where some papers diverge from the survey, is emotional neglect on the binary total.

### Cited Findings
- The ACE-IQ childhood questions are grouped into 13 categories: emotional abuse; physical abuse; sexual abuse; violence against household members; living with a household member who was a substance abuser; living with a household member who was mentally ill or suicidal; living with a household member who was imprisoned; one or no parents, parental separation or divorce; emotional neglect; physical neglect; bullying; community violence; collective violence. Both the binary and the frequency method produce a total from 0 to 13. — [WHO ACE-IQ guidance for analysing](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/documents/child-maltreatment/ace-iq-guidance-for-analysing.pdf)
- Binary method: an affirmative answer counts, "whether with once, a few times, or many times." Yes/no items count a yes. — [WHO guidance](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/documents/child-maltreatment/ace-iq-guidance-for-analysing.pdf)
- Frequency method, higher threshold, from the same guidance's Table 2:
  - Many times is required for physical abuse (spanked/beaten, or hit with an object), emotional abuse (yelled at/humiliated, or threatened with abandonment), yelling/insulting a household member, physical neglect (not enough food, caregiver too intoxicated to provide care, not sent to school), bullying, and community violence (beaten, stabbed or shot, or threatened with a knife or gun).
  - A few times or many times is required for seeing a household member slapped, kicked, punched or beaten, and for seeing a household member hit or cut with an object.
  - Sexual abuse counts any occurrence ("ever"). Collective violence counts any occurrence. Household substance use, household mental illness, incarceration, and parental separation or death are yes/no and use the same rule in both totals.
  - Emotional neglect: parents/guardians "rarely or never" understood problems and worries, or "rarely or never" knew what the child was doing in free time. — [WHO guidance](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/documents/child-maltreatment/ace-iq-guidance-for-analysing.pdf)
- On the binary table, emotional neglect is the exception to "any affirmative counts." The note says the "no" answer scores 1. The guidance does not say whether "sometimes" counts as that "no." The frequency table is explicit: rarely or never. — [WHO guidance](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/documents/child-maltreatment/ace-iq-guidance-for-analysing.pdf)
- The questionnaire is designed for people aged 18 and older. Questions ask about experiences while growing up. — [WHO ACE-IQ publication page](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/adverse-childhood-experiences-international-questionnaire-(ace-iq))
- The instrument is described as about 29 items covering the 13 categories, not a 13-item checklist. The total is a count of categories, not a sum of frequency ratings. — [Chen et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42844-022-00076-8)
- WHO recommends calculating both scores at this pilot stage and examining whether a health association appears with the binary score or only with the frequency score. The guidance does not define a clinical cut-point. It points readers to the CDC ACE study website for examples of dose-response analysis. — [WHO guidance](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/documents/child-maltreatment/ace-iq-guidance-for-analysing.pdf)

### Inferences
- The survey's 13 categories are the WHO 13. The survey's frequency rules match Table 2 of the guidance, including "many times" for emotional abuse, physical abuse, yelling at a household member, physical neglect, bullying, and community violence, and "a few times or many times" for seeing a household member slapped or hit with an object.
- The survey uses "rarely or never" for the two parent-relationship items on both totals. That matches the frequency rule. It matches the binary rule only if "no" is read as rarely or never, and not as sometimes/rarely/never. Papers that count "sometimes" as binary emotional neglect are not on the survey's binary metric. Their binary totals will run higher than the survey's binary total for the same answers.
- A 10-category ACE score, an 8-item youth ACE module, or an extended ACE list that is not these 13 categories cannot be translated onto either survey total.

### Gaps
- The original ACE-IQ response card for the parent items was not re-quoted here beyond the analysis guidance. The binary "no" remains ambiguous in that guidance.

## Distributions in general-population or school samples

### Takeaway
No European general-population or school sample was found that reports the percent with 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more ACE-IQ categories, separately for binary and frequency scoring, on the full 0–13 metric. The figures below are the closest published totals. Several are not valid translations onto the survey.

### Cited Findings
- A 2022 systematic review of ACE-IQ in community samples (63 documents) concluded that, on average, 75% of community respondents experienced ACEs, with a mean of three, primarily emotional abuse and bullying. Males experienced more ACEs but were underrepresented, as were children and adolescents. Most studies were in Asia or Africa. The review records a lack of studies in Europe, the Americas, and Oceania, and a lack of reporting of prevalence or means. — [Pace, Muzi, Rogier, Meinero, and Marcenaro, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2022.105640)
- A later psychometric paper, citing that review, states that none of the studies in it compared binary and frequency scoring, and that some studies did not specify the method. — [Vallejos, Sánchez, and Bühring, 2025, Children](https://doi.org/10.3390/children12030340); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11941045/)
- Malawian adolescents, the clearest youth study that used both WHO methods on 13 domains. Rural Mchinji District, July 2017, n = 410, age 10–16 (mean 12.99, SD 1.74), 47% female, 94% attending school. Caregivers had low schooling and the households had few assets; adult HIV prevalence in rural Malawi was 7.5%. This is a low-income, high-HIV sample, not a European school norm. Binary total: mean 6.2, median 6.0, range 0–12; over 99% reported at least one ACE; nearly 30% reported more than 7. Frequency total, using the WHO frequency guidance: mean 2.9, median 3.0. The paper does not report the percent at 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 or more for either total. Binary category endorsements included emotional neglect 91%, community violence 99%, emotional abuse 71%, domestic violence 70%, physical abuse 68%, parents dead or divorced 56%, bullying 51%, physical neglect 39%, household substance use 31%, incarceration 17%, collective violence 17%, household mental illness 7%, sexual abuse 6%. — [Kidman, Smith, Piccolo, and Kohler, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2019.03.015); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6513701/)
- A later wave of the same Malawi cohort (adolescents interviewed at baseline and follow-up, exposure counted if reported at either wave) gives a mean of 6.74 ACEs and "nearly 90%" at 4 or more. That score is a two-wave cumulative count of 13 binary domains, not a single administration, and the paper does not present a parallel frequency total. — [Kidman, Breton, Behrman, Rui, and Kohler, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2024.106895)
- Chinese health-science students, Shanghai, May–August 2020, n = 566, age 18–38, mean about 22 (Table 2 mean 21.92, SD 2.67; text also says SD 2.83), 70.1% female. Label: young adult students, not adolescents. The paper scored 12 categories, total 0–12, not 13. Binary: mean 3.10 (SD 2.07), range 0–10; 65/566 (11.5%) scored 0; 88.5% at least one; 42.6% four or more. Frequency: mean 1.42 (SD 1.64), range 0–10; 209/566 (36.9%) scored 0; 63.1% at least one; 12.2% four or more. The percent at exactly 1, 2, and 3 was not reported. Emotional neglect was 31.6% on the frequency method and 2.1% on the binary method, which is the reverse of the WHO threshold logic and indicates that this study's binary neglect coding does not match the survey. — [Chen, Yu, Wang, and Gross, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42844-022-00076-8)
- Chilean community adults in the MAUCO cohort, n = 651, age 37–66, mean 48.4 (SD 6.8), 53.5% women. Label: middle-aged adults. Both methods, 13 categories. Binary mean 5.4 (SD 2.9); frequency mean 3.0 (SD 2.7). Women binary 5.7 (SD 2.9), frequency 3.4 (SD 2.8); men binary 5.1 (SD 2.9), frequency 2.5 (SD 2.4). No distribution of 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 or more. Emotional neglect was 59.1% binary and 28.7% frequency. Sexual abuse, household substance use, household mental illness, incarceration, caregiver death/separation, and collective violence were identical under both methods (26.1%, 34.9%, 10.9%, 7.1%, 32.1%, 31.2%). Categories that dropped under frequency scoring included physical abuse 63.0% to 15.5%, emotional abuse 54.5% to 19.0%, bullying 48.7% to 14.6%, community violence 71.9% to 15.7%, domestic violence 67.3% to 43.8%, physical neglect 34.9% to 16.3%. — [Vallejos et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/children12030340)
- Mexican schooled adolescents (n = 5,835, mean age 16.13) used an extended ACE-IQ reorganised into a final 16 adversities, not the WHO 13. Ninety percent had one or more, and about a third had six or more. — [Casas-Muñoz et al. abstract as indexed, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2023.106492). This is not a 0–13 ACE-IQ total.
- The Dutch ACE-IQ-10 is a 10-item form in clinical samples. It is not the 13-category total. — [paper PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/199356/8/The_10_item_Adverse_Childhood_Experience_International_Questionnaire_ACE_IQ_10_psychometric_properties_of_the_Dutch_version_in_two_clinical_samples.pdf). Not used as a norm.
- US high-school estimates such as 22.4% with 4 or more ACEs come from an 8-item Youth Risk Behavior Survey module, not the ACE-IQ. — [Swedo et al. abstract, Pediatrics](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2024-066633). Not an ACE-IQ norm.

### Inferences
- There is no published European adolescent distribution (percent at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more, plus the mean) on the survey's binary total or the survey's frequency total.
- Kidman et al. (2019) is a valid comparison of the two WHO methods in adolescents, on a 0–13 count, but the setting (rural Malawi, high HIV, very high community violence) makes the binary mean of 6.2 a high-adversity figure, not a European community benchmark. The frequency mean of 2.9 in that same sample shows how far the two survey totals can sit apart. The missing 0/1/2/3/4+ split cannot be reconstructed from the mean and median.
- Chen et al. (2022) is the clearest side-by-side prevalence of "four or more," but the total is 0–12 and the binary neglect coding does not match the survey. Translating 42.6% and 12.2% onto the survey's 0–13 totals is not valid.
- The Chilean means are on a 13-category instrument in adults. Because binary emotional neglect (59.1%) is about double the frequency rate (28.7%), their binary rule for that category was looser than "rarely or never." The frequency mean of 3.0 (SD 2.7) is closer to the survey's frequency total. The binary mean of 5.4 is not a clean translation onto the survey's binary total. Adult means are a weak comparison for a youth survey and are labelled as adult.
- Pace et al.'s community average of "75% any ACE, mean of three" mixes scoring methods and regions. It is not a norm for either survey total.
- The Mexican 16-adversity score, the Dutch 10-item form, and the US 8-item youth module are different instruments. Their prevalences are not ACE-IQ norms.

### Gaps
- Percent at exactly 0, 1, 2, and 3 was not found for any sample on both WHO methods.
- No European school or general-population adolescent ACE-IQ mean, binary or frequency, was found. Pace et al. (2022) and Pace et al. (2024) both say European ACE-IQ data are too sparse to pool.

## Clinical or high-risk comparison

### Takeaway
On the binary 0–13 ACE-IQ, Polish adult women in treatment scored about 6, against about 2.8 in a comparison group of women without adaptation problems. That is an adult, binary-only, European clinical contrast. Adolescent clinical ACE-IQ means on the survey's two metrics were not found. Pooled clinical reviews mix scoring methods.

### Cited Findings
- Gąsior and Chodkiewicz (2020) gave the WHO ACE-IQ, binary version, 13 categories, score 0–13, to Polish women. Internalising disorders (anxiety, mixed anxiety-depression), n = 79: mean 6.46 (SD 2.42). Externalising problems (addiction, including 23 women both addicted and incarcerated), n = 80: mean 6.05 (SD 3.22). The two clinical means did not differ (t = −0.87, p = .390). A comparison group of women without adaptation problems (part-time students and employees), n = 129: mean 2.84 (SD 2.13). The clinical groups differed from the comparison group. — [Gąsior and Chodkiewicz, 2020, PDF](https://cipp.ug.edu.pl/pdf-122262-73723?filename=Adverse-childhood-experie.pdf)
- The same paper states that the clinical means were about twice the comparison-group mean, and that the comparison mean was "below the 3rd category." Ages in a later review's extraction: internalising mean 34.9 (SD 9.93) and externalising mean 39.8 (SD 10.62), range 19–68, 0% male. Those ages are from the review table, not re-calculated here. — [Pace, Muzi, Rogier, Ballestracci, and Mazzola, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/car.2883)
- Pace et al. (2024) pooled clinical and at-risk ACE-IQ studies (23 studies): an average mean of 4.93 and 81.74% with any ACE. Clinical prevalence in the studies that reported it ranged from 77% to 98%. Means in clinical samples ranged from 1.71 to 6.46. The review states that studies often do not say whether binary or frequency scoring was used, that only one European study in the set reported a prevalence, and that at-risk ACE-IQ samples are missing in Europe. Five included studies involved minors; the authors note that adaptation of the adult ACE-IQ for children and adolescents was not documented. Underage category prevalences that could be pooled came from low-income, high-HIV, and refugee samples, and "there were no data on means" for those underage pools. — [Pace et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/car.2883)
- Kidman et al. (2019), already cited, is classed by that review as an at-risk adolescent sample (low income, high HIV), not as a clinic sample. Binary mean 6.2, frequency mean 2.9. — [Kidman et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2019.03.015); [Pace et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/car.2883)

### Inferences
- For a European comparison on the binary 0–13 total, the Polish adult figures are usable only with the adult label: clinical means 6.05 and 6.46 versus 2.84 in women without adaptation problems. They are not youth norms, and there is no frequency total in that paper.
- The pooled clinical mean of 4.93 and the 81.74% "any ACE" figure should not be placed next to a survey score. The scoring method is not consistent across the pooled studies.
- A child-welfare, refugee, or adolescent mental-health-clinic distribution on the survey's binary and frequency totals was not established. Malawi adolescents are the high-adversity youth comparison that does use both methods.

### Gaps
- No child-welfare or CAMHS sample with both WHO totals on the 13-category ACE-IQ was retrieved.
- Refugee ACE-IQ papers appear in the Pace et al. (2024) tables (Silove et al., 2018; Tay et al., 2021) but the fetched text did not give a 0–13 mean that could be checked against the survey's rules. Those means are not reported here.

## The status of "4 or more"

### Takeaway
"Four or more" is a threshold from the original US ACE study, which is a shorter instrument than the 13-category ACE-IQ. WHO does not define 4 or more as the ACE-IQ cut. Some ACE-IQ papers still use it. On a young-adult 12-category version, binary scoring put 42.6% at 4 or more and frequency scoring put 12.2% there. That pair is not a valid 13-category norm. A 10-item or 8-item ACE prevalence is not an ACE-IQ norm.

### Cited Findings
- The original ACE study associated the number of categories with later health outcomes. The familiar high-exposure marker of 4 or more comes from that study's instrument (abuse and household dysfunction), not from the ACE-IQ's 13 categories. — The assignment states this; the WHO guidance itself sends readers to the CDC ACE pages for dose-response examples and does not set 4 as a cut. — [WHO guidance](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/documents/child-maltreatment/ace-iq-guidance-for-analysing.pdf). Primary citation for the original study, not re-extracted for a prevalence in this note: Felitti et al., 1998, American Journal of Preventive Medicine, [DOI 10.1016/S0749-3797(98)00017-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0749-3797(98)00017-8).
- Chen et al. (2022) applied "four or more" to their 12-category ACE-IQ and reported 42.6% (binary) and 12.2% (frequency). They note that the binary 4+ rate was nearly 3.5 times the frequency rate. — [Chen et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42844-022-00076-8)
- Kidman et al. (2024) applied a "≥4" threshold, "used to denote high exposure in prior studies," to Malawian adolescents and found nearly 90% at or above it, on a two-wave cumulative binary count (mean 6.74). — [Kidman et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2024.106895)
- The Chilean ACE-IQ paper mentions that a national survey found around 40% of the urban population with four or more ACEs. It does not identify that survey as the 13-category ACE-IQ scored with either WHO method. The figure is not used here as an ACE-IQ prevalence. — [Vallejos et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/children12030340)
- Pace et al. (2024) describe both WHO systems as producing the same 0–13 range and criticise the frequency method for not actually scoring how frequent each adversity was. They do not treat 4 or more as part of the WHO scoring rules. — [Pace et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/car.2883)

### Inferences
- Papers do apply 4 or more to ACE-IQ totals. The prevalence depends on the scoring method. In the one study that published both, binary 4+ was 42.6% and frequency 4+ was 12.2%, but that study omitted a category and miscoded neglect relative to the survey, so those percents are not survey norms.
- Because the ACE-IQ adds bullying, community violence, and collective violence to the household adversities, a score of 4 on the ACE-IQ is not the same exposure set as a score of 4 on the original US ACE questionnaire. Using 4 or more as if it were a validated ACE-IQ risk threshold is not supported by the WHO guidance.
- A 10-item ACE prevalence, including the original expanded CDC questionnaire and the Dutch ACE-IQ-10, is a different denominator. It is not reported here as a benchmark for the survey.

### Gaps
- No European adolescent paper was found that reports the percent with 4 or more of the 13 ACE-IQ categories under either WHO method.

# KIDSCREEN-10

## Items, reversals, and why the survey sum is not a T-score

### Takeaway
The survey's 10 items are the standard KIDSCREEN-10 index. Official scoring is 1 to 5 per item, raw sum 10 to 50, with "felt sad" and "felt lonely" reversed, then a Rasch T-score. If the anchors line up, survey sum + 10 equals that raw sum. The survey does not compute the T-score, and a raw sum must not be read as one.

### Cited Findings
- The KIDSCREEN-10 has 10 items, each on a 5-point scale, referring to the last week. The 2010 validity paper lists them as: (1) Have you felt fit and well? (2) Have you felt full of energy? (3) Have you felt sad? (4) Have you felt lonely? (5) Have you had enough time for yourself? (6) Have you been able to do the things that you want to do in your free time? (7) Have your parent(s) treated you fairly? (8) Have you had fun with your friends? (9) Have you got on well at school? (10) Have you been able to pay attention? Items 1 and 9 use not at all / slightly / moderately / very / extremely. The other items use never / seldom / quite often / very often / always. — [Ravens-Sieberer et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-010-9706-5); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2977059/)
- Negatively worded items are recoded so that higher scores mean higher health-related quality of life. For the KIDSCREEN-10 index the manual's recode list is items 3 and 4: "Have you felt sad?" and "Have you felt lonely?" Item scores then run from 1 to 5. — [KIDSCREEN manual, English PDF, Table 30 and scoring steps](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/0/module/12416751312/name/KIDSCREEN_manual_En%20glish.pdf)
- The raw sum of the 10 index items runs from 10 to 50. The general-health item that sometimes accompanies the questionnaire is not part of the index. A sum is calculated by hand only when every index item is answered. — [KIDSCREEN Group, evaluation by hand](https://www.kidscreen.org/english/analysis/evaluation-by-hand/)
- The raw sum is ordinal. A Rasch person parameter is assigned to each raw sum from 10 to 50, then standardised to a T-score with mean 50 and SD 10 in the international (European) reference sample. The manual states that the person parameter is a non-linear transformation of the sum score: nearly linear in the middle of the scale, and steeper at the extremes. — [KIDSCREEN manual](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/0/module/12416751312/name/KIDSCREEN_manual_En%20glish.pdf); [evaluation by hand](https://www.kidscreen.org/english/analysis/evaluation-by-hand/)
- Published example of that international conversion: a self-report raw sum of 36 corresponds to Rasch person parameter 0.52 and T = 43.35. — [evaluation by hand](https://www.kidscreen.org/english/analysis/evaluation-by-hand/)
- The manual's individual-norm table confirms the linear 0–100 rescaling of the raw sum: raw 10 = 0, raw 35 = 62.50, raw 50 = 100. That is (raw − 10) / 40 × 100. The same table's T column is not one international T. At raw 35 the printed T differs by column (for example 37.2 in children 8–11 overall, 44.0 in adolescents 12–18 overall, 42.4 in ages 8–18 overall). The footnote defines those T-scores as "for the particular (sub-)population (M = 50, SD = 10)." — [KIDSCREEN manual, Appendix Table A2-16](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/0/module/12416751312/name/KIDSCREEN_manual_En%20glish.pdf)
- The group-norm appendix uses a different T: "international T-values based on Rasch person parameter." On that metric the combined self-report reference sample sits at mean 50 and SD 10 by construction. — [European normdata, Appendix A7_A](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/1709645508/module/12417032512/name/A7_A_european_normdata_group-level_comparisons.pdf)

### Inferences
- The survey items (fit and well, full of energy, sad, lonely, time for yourself, free time, parents treated you fairly, fun with friends, got on well at school, able to pay attention) are the KIDSCREEN-10 index, in the same order as Ravens-Sieberer et al. (2010). The two reversed items are the same two the manual recodes.
- Survey sum + 10 = official raw sum only if: each survey point 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 maps onto official points 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 in the same order; sad and lonely are reversed before the sum in both systems; and all 10 items are answered. The official anchors are not the same words on every item (two items use an intensity scale, eight use a frequency scale). The +10 shift assumes the survey preserved that order. It does not assume the survey computed a Rasch score.
- A survey score is a raw sum on 0–40. It is not a T-score. Adding 10 produces the official 10–50 raw sum. It still does not produce a T-score. The international T is a non-linear function of that raw sum, so a T cannot be obtained by rescaling 0–40 or 10–50 with a straight line.

### Gaps
- The Excel file "Normdata_Raw-score_Rasch_T-Value" is linked from kidscreen.org but was not opened as a spreadsheet. The international T is therefore known for raw 36 (43.35) and not for every other raw sum. No T was interpolated.

## European reference, low quality of life, community and clinical means, and a score of 25 out of 40

### Takeaway
The European reference is the KIDSCREEN project in 13 countries, ages 8–18, T-scores set to mean 50 and SD 10 in that pooled self-report sample. Adolescents aged 12–18 score lower: international T mean 48.51 (SD 9.28). Authors treat T below about 40 as low health-related quality of life; that is a rule of thumb, and a German analysis put the optimal older-adolescent cut at 40.29. A survey score of 25 out of 40 is an official raw score of 35. Among European adolescents that raw score is the 28.3rd percentile. The international Rasch T for raw 35 was not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Reference sample. The manual's individual norms are titled "European reference population (13 countries)." The computer version lists the languages as Czech, Dutch, English (UK and Ireland), French, German (Germany, Switzerland, Austria), Greek, Hungarian, Spanish, Polish, and Swedish, which is those 13 countries. Some scoring pages say the international T-values rest on "twelve European countries." The norm tables themselves say 13. — [KIDSCREEN manual, section 5.6 and Table A2-16](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/0/module/12416751312/name/KIDSCREEN_manual_En%20glish.pdf); [evaluation by hand](https://www.kidscreen.org/english/analysis/evaluation-by-hand/)
- Ravens-Sieberer et al. (2010) tested the index in 22,830 European children and adolescents aged 8–18 and 16,237 parents. Self-report Cronbach's alpha was 0.82 (0.81 at ages 12–18). Test-retest ICC was 0.70 overall and 0.69 at ages 12–18. — [Ravens-Sieberer et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-010-9706-5)
- Group norms, self-report, international T and the 0–100 sum score. Adolescents 12–18, sexes combined: n = 14,932, T mean 48.51 (SD 9.28), 0–100 mean 71.93 (SD 14.95). Girls 12–18: n = 7,938, T mean 47.21 (SD 8.98), 0–100 mean 69.72 (SD 15.34). Boys 12–18: n = 6,970, T mean 49.97 (SD 9.40), 0–100 mean 74.43 (SD 14.09). Children 8–11, sexes combined: n = 5,715, T mean 53.90 (SD 10.73). Ages 8–18 self-report, the sample on which the international T is fixed: n = 20,647, T mean 50.00 (SD 10.00), 0–100 mean 74.07 (SD 14.94). — [Appendix A7_A](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/1709645508/module/12417032512/name/A7_A_european_normdata_group-level_comparisons.pdf)
- The 2010 paper's age contrast, self-report: ages 8–11, T mean 54.40 (SD 10.67); ages 12–18, T mean 47.97 (SD 9.16); effect size d = 0.64. Girls 8–18, T mean 49.02 (SD 9.90); boys, 50.90 (SD 9.92); d = 0.19 overall and d = 0.28 in the 12–18 column of that table. Country means in that paper are for ages 8–18, adjusted for age and gender, not adolescent-only: Germany 51.95 (SD 9.55), Spain 52.65 (SD 10.95), Netherlands 53.88 (SD 10.40), Austria 53.05 (SD 10.36), France 46.80 (SD 9.04), United Kingdom 47.55 (SD 9.75), Switzerland 52.75 (SD 9.24), Hungary 47.95 (SD 10.33). The paper says the highest self-report country means were the Netherlands (53.9) and Austria (53.1), and the lowest were France (46.8) and Poland (46.8). — [Ravens-Sieberer et al., 2010, Tables 5 and 6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2977059/)
- What "low" means. The KIDSCREEN Group's analysis FAQ: the instrument is not clinical and has no healthy-versus-ill cut. For interpretation they still write that T-scores below 40 can be taken as low quality of life and T-scores above 60 as high. A separate group rule is "reference value ± ½ standard deviation" as the average band. — [KIDSCREEN FAQ, analysis](https://www.kidscreen.org/english/faq/analysis/)
- A re-analysis of the German KiGGS survey estimated optimal epidemiological cuts against the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire, self-rated general health, and chronic disease. Classification against mental-health problems was acceptable (AUCs 0.77 to 0.79). Cuts against chronic disease were not. Optimal cuts: parent report 41.13; younger adolescents' self-report 42.52; older adolescents' self-report 40.29. The authors suggest using 41, 42, and 40 respectively. — [Quality of Life Research, 2020, DOI 10.1007/s11136-019-02324-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-019-02324-4); [Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31620984)
- Clinical and health contrasts on the international T, from the 2010 European sample, self-report. Means below are the ages 8–18 column. The 12–18 entries in that table, as printed, are effect sizes, not separate means. Child-reported SDQ, normal: T 49.74 (SD 9.00), n = 11,537. Borderline: T 41.96 (SD 6.67), n = 1,342. Abnormal (probable mental-health problems): T 40.08 (SD 8.37), n = 711. Effect size, normal versus abnormal, d = 1.06 for ages 8–18 and d = 1.06 in the 12–18 column. Parent-reported SDQ abnormal, with the child's own KIDSCREEN: T 44.17 (SD 8.60), d = 0.67 overall and d = 0.68 in the 12–18 column. Special health care needs (parent CSHCN screener): no condition T 50.52 (SD 10.01); some condition T 47.31 (SD 9.18); d = 0.32 overall and d = 0.32 in the 12–18 column. — [Ravens-Sieberer et al., 2010, Table 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2977059/)
- The manual summarises the same known-groups pattern as effect sizes without reprinting those means: low family affluence d = 0.47, behavioural problems on the SDQ d = 1.30, high psychosomatic complaints d = 1.69. — [KIDSCREEN manual, section 5.4](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/0/module/12416751312/name/KIDSCREEN_manual_En%20glish.pdf)
- Placing raw 35, from Table A2-16, European adolescents 12–18, sexes combined: percentile 28.3, within-that-group T 44.0. Girls 12–18: percentile 33.7, within-group T 45.6. Boys 12–18: percentile 21.9, within-group T 41.7. Ages 8–18 combined: percentile 23.6, within-group T 42.4. These within-group T values are not the international Rasch T. The international anchor that was published in prose is raw 36 = T 43.35. — [KIDSCREEN manual, Table A2-16](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/0/module/12416751312/name/KIDSCREEN_manual_En%20glish.pdf); [evaluation by hand](https://www.kidscreen.org/english/analysis/evaluation-by-hand/)

### Inferences
- Prefer the appendix group norms for adolescent means. They are unstandardised descriptions of the reference sample on the international T. The 2010 paper's adolescent mean (47.97, SD 9.16) is close to the appendix (48.51, SD 9.28). The small difference is left unresolved; the appendix is the norm table, and the paper's country means are adjusted.
- Community raw sum on the official 10–50 scale, adolescents 12–18: the appendix does not print it. It prints the 0–100 mean 71.93. The manual table shows that 0–100 = (raw − 10) / 40 × 100. Under that identity, 71.93 corresponds to raw 10 + 28.772 = 38.8 on the 10–50 scale. Girls: 0–100 mean 69.72 corresponds to raw 37.9. Boys: 0–100 mean 74.43 corresponds to raw 39.8. These three raw means are calculated here; they are not printed as 10–50 means in the sources. The same identity applied to the survey's 0–40 metric (survey = raw − 10) puts the adolescent combined mean at 28.8 out of 40, girls at 27.9, boys at 29.8.
- Ages 8–18 combined, 0–100 mean 74.07, corresponds to raw 39.6 on 10–50 under the same identity. That figure includes children, who score higher, and should not be used as the adolescent benchmark.
- Low quality of life, on the T metric the authors actually use: below 40 on the KIDSCREEN Group's own FAQ, and 40.29 (suggested cut 40) for older adolescents in the German KiGGS analysis. Both are T-scores. Neither is a raw-sum cut. The FAQ also says the questionnaire does not separate healthy from ill.
- Mental-health comparison, same international T metric, ages 8–18 because age-specific means were not printed: probable cases on the self-report SDQ averaged T 40.08, about 10 points below the normal group (49.74), a large effect that the paper also reports at ages 12–18 (d = 1.06). Chronic or special health care needs shifted the mean only to 47.31 (d = 0.32 at 12–18 as well as overall). The mental-health contrast is the relevant clinical comparison. These T means were not converted to raw scores. The conversion is non-linear, and the inverse table was not retrieved.
- A survey score of 25 out of 40. Assumption: the +10 shift is valid, so the official raw sum is 35 and the 0–100 score is 62.5. Among European adolescents aged 12–18, raw 35 is the 28.3rd percentile (33.7th in girls, 21.9th in boys). The manual's within-adolescent T for that raw score is 44.0. That is not the international T used in papers. The nearest published international T is 43.35 at raw 36, so a raw score of 35 lies below that anchor. No source retrieved in this search states the international T for raw 35, and none was calculated by interpolation. On the survey's own 0–40 metric, 25 sits 3.8 points under the derived adolescent mean of 28.8. It is above the midpoint of the 0–40 scale (20) and below the community mean. It should not be described as a T-score of 25, and it should not be described as under the "T below 40" low band unless an international T is obtained from the Rasch table.

### Gaps
- International Rasch T for official raw 35 (survey 25) was not retrieved. The downloadable conversion spreadsheet was not opened.
- Age-specific (12–18) mean T-scores for the SDQ-abnormal and CSHCN groups were not printed in the 2010 table that was extracted; only the 8–18 means and the 12–18 effect sizes were.
- A clinical sample reporting the KIDSCREEN-10 as a raw sum on 10–50 or 0–40, rather than a T-score, was not found. Clinical placement of a survey raw score therefore goes through the T metric, not through a published raw-score clinical mean.

# CYRM-12

## Items, metric, community means, cut-offs, lower groups, and sex differences

### Takeaway
The survey's 12 items are the Liebenberg, Ungar, and LeBlanc CYRM-12. Published 5-point forms sum to 12–60 with no reversals. Survey sum + 12 equals that sum if the anchors match. A school sample of Turkish 10–14-year-olds averaged 49.47 (SD 8.08) on 12–60, which is 37.47 on the survey's 0–48 metric. There is no standard low-resilience cut. The Resilience Research Centre says not to use one. A sample median used as a cut in one small study is not the standard. No published mean for youth in care or in clinic, on this 12-item 12–60 form, was found.

### Cited Findings
- The 12 items retained in the original reduction are: I have people I look up to; Getting an education is important to me; My parent(s)/caregiver(s) know a lot about me; I try to finish what I start; I solve problems without harming myself or others (for example by using drugs and/or being violent); I know where to go in my community to get help; I feel I belong at my school; My family stands by me during difficult times; My friends stand by me during difficult times; I am treated fairly in my community; I have opportunities to develop skills that will be useful later in life; I enjoy my cultural and family traditions. — [Liebenberg, Ungar, and LeBlanc, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf03405676); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6974279/)
- Development samples, both Atlantic Canada. Multiple-service-using youth (child welfare, mental health, juvenile justice, special education, and community programmes): n = 122, age 14–22, mean 18 (SD 2.017), 37% female. This sample was used to choose the 12 items. School sample, grades 7–12, rural and urban public schools: the abstract says n = 1,494, mean age 15; the methods say 1,574 students aged 10–18, mean 15 (SD 1.715), 862 (53%) girls, with the confirmatory analysis reported as n = 1,494. Alpha for the 12 items in the school sample was 0.840. The paper does not report a total-score mean or SD for either sample. — [Liebenberg et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf03405676)
- Scoring. Items are positively worded. There are no reverse-coded items. A 5-point form is summed. Current Centre guidance gives the minimum and maximum for the revised 17-item measure (17–85 on a 1–5 scale), and says the minimum and maximum of modified measures may vary. Higher scores mean more of the resources the scale asks about. — [Resilience Research Centre, CYRM and ARM user manual v2.5, 2022, PDF](https://resilienceresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/CYRM-ARM-user-manual-V2.5.pdf)
- The Centre now recommends the 17-item CYRM-R rather than the CYRM-12. On cut-offs, the manual says the Centre has been asked for a score that means "good" or "normal" resilience and does not provide one, because any threshold would vary by context. The suggested alternative is to contrast high and low scorers inside the user's own sample, or to call scores more than one SD above the sample mean "higher," scores within one SD "moderate," and scores below one SD "low." The manual says norms are not well established. That ±1 SD rule is a within-sample description, not a published absolute cut, and the scoring section around it is written for the revised measures. — [RRC manual v2.5](https://resilienceresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/CYRM-ARM-user-manual-V2.5.pdf)
- School mean on the 12–60 metric. Turkish secondary-school students, n = 501, age 10–14 (259 girls, 51.7%; 242 boys, 48.3%), five schools. The paper states that the CYRM-12 is rated from "Describes me a lot (5)" to "Does not describe me at all (1)," that the total runs from 12 to 60, and that there is no reverse coding. Resilience mean 49.47 (SD 8.08). — [Çıtak, Kanbur, and Kurşuncu, 2023](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1407151.pdf), International Journal of Psychology and Educational Studies, 10(3), 773–786.
- European young adults, not adolescents, and reported as an item mean rather than a sum. Slovenian online panel, March–April 2024, final n = 330, age 18–24, mean 21.2 (SD 1.85), 63.3% female. Five-point scale, 1 "Does not describe me at all" to 5 "Describes me a lot." Items 1–11 match the Liebenberg wording. Item 12 is printed as "I enjoy my community's traditions," not "I enjoy my cultural and family traditions." The scale mean in the correlation table is 3.76 (SD 0.68). The average of the 12 printed item means is 3.76, so 3.76 is the mean item score, and 0.68 is the SD of that person-level mean, not the SD of a 12–60 sum. Alpha 0.87. — [Frontiers in Psychology, 19 February 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1467174); correspondence address names Urška Smrke.
- Sex difference that is not large. Iranian middle- and high-school students, Islamshahr, ages 14–18, mean 16.69 (SD 0.94), n = 440 (178 boys, 262 girls). The paper scores the CYRM-12 from not at all (1) to a lot (5), range 12–60. Boys and girls did not differ: t(438) = −0.04, p = 0.966. The total mean was not in the text retrieved. — [Ghahremani, Fathali Lavasani, Moinalghorabaei, Dehghani, and colleagues](https://brieflands.com/journals/ijpbs/articles/114635)
- A different 12-item scale, not the survey's items. Panter-Brick and colleagues tested the standard one-factor CYRM-12 in Syrian refugee and Jordanian host-community adolescents (n = 603, ages 11–18) and report poor fit (CFI = 0.85). They then built their own 12-item, three-factor short form. That form includes items that are not in the Liebenberg 12, including "I am aware of my own strengths," "My parent(s)/caregiver(s) watch me closely," and "I feel safe when I am with my family." The mean 50.30 (SD 6.71), range 25–60, belongs to the scale they validated in that paper. It is not a mean on the survey's 12 items. — [Panter-Brick et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12868)
- Cuts that are not the standard. A Kolkata school study of 151 students aged 12–14 scored each CYRM-12 item 1–3, so the total runs from 12 to 36, and used the sample median of 31 as a cut (57/151, 37.7%, above 31 called resilient). — [Dasgupta and colleagues, as published in the journal PDF](https://www.ijpediatrics.com/index.php/ijcp/article/download/1738/1287). A Tehran school study of 653 students reported a resilience mean of 27.8 (SD 4.1) with minimum 12 and maximum 36, which is a 1–3 metric, not 1–5. — [Persian CYRM-12 psychometric paper, Health in Disasters Quarterly PDF](https://hdq.uswr.ac.ir/article-1-498-en.pdf). A Slovak HBSC thesis used a 1–3 response scale with adolescents aged 13–15 and did not recommend the scale; item means on that 1–3 metric are not convertible. — [thesis PDF](https://psych.fss.muni.cz/media/3623906/soutez-2024-anna-literova.pdf)

### Inferences
- The survey item list matches Liebenberg et al. (2013), including "enjoy cultural and family traditions." No item is reversed on the published form or on the survey. Survey sum + 12 = official 12–60 sum if the survey's 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 are the same five ordered anchors as the published 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Anchor words vary slightly across papers ("not at all" versus "does not describe me at all"; "a lot" versus "describes me a lot"). The +12 shift assumes the survey used one of these 5-point intensity anchors, not a 3-point scale.
- Turkish school mean, ages 10–14: 49.47 (SD 8.08) on 12–60. On the survey's 0–48 metric that is 49.47 − 12 = 37.47, and the SD is unchanged at 8.08, because subtracting a constant does not change the SD. This is the best adolescent school figure found on the matching metric. It is a Turkish school sample, not a Western European general-population norm. Sex-specific means were not extracted from that paper.
- Slovenian young adults, ages 18–24: mean item score 3.76 (SD 0.68). If each person's score was the average of the 12 items, the 12–60 sum mean is 3.76 × 12 = 45.12 and the sum SD is 0.68 × 12 = 8.16. On the survey's 0–48 metric that sum mean is 45.12 − 12 = 33.12, SD 8.16. Both the multiplication and the item-12 wording ("community's traditions") are assumptions. This sample is older than the survey's likely age range and is an online panel, not a school. It is labelled young adult.
- Liebenberg et al. (2013) do not provide a number that can be converted. The multiple-service-using youth are the lower, high-adversity development sample, but their 12-item total was not published. Item means printed in that paper are from the 28-item pool during item reduction, not a CYRM-12 total. They are not summed here.
- Panter-Brick's mean of 50.30 must not be converted. The items are not the survey's 12. The Kolkata median of 31 and the Tehran mean of 27.8 must not be converted. Those studies used 3-point items (totals 12–36). Adding or subtracting 12 does not put a 3-point scale onto a 5-point scale.
- No standard low-resilience cut exists for the CYRM-12. The Centre's manual refuses a universal cut. The Kolkata median of 31 is a cut one small study made on a different response scale. It is not the standard. The ±1 SD suggestion is relative to whatever sample the user has, and in the manual it sits with the revised 17-item measure. It is not a fixed score such as "below 40."
- The one sex comparison with a test statistic, Iranian students aged 14–18 on the 1–5 CYRM-12, was null (t = −0.04). That is not a large sex difference. Means by sex were not retrieved, so the size of any difference in other samples is not stated.

### Gaps
- Liebenberg et al. (2013) published no school mean. A Canadian or Western European school mean on the standard 12-item, 12–60 CYRM-12 was not found.
- No mean for youth in care, in a mental-health clinic, or in another high-adversity service sample was found on the standard 12 items scored 12–60. The multiple-service sample exists, but the total score does not.
- Sex-specific means for the Turkish school sample were not extracted. The Slovenian paper does not report a sum mean by sex.
- Ghahremani and colleagues' Iranian total mean, which would have been on the 12–60 metric, was not in the retrieved text.

# Figures that must not be used as survey norms

### Takeaway
Several widely cited numbers are the wrong instrument, the wrong number of categories, the wrong response scale, or a T-score being treated as a raw sum.

### Cited Findings
- Original US ACE 4+ threshold: different and shorter category list than the ACE-IQ. — [Felitti et al., 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0749-3797(98)00017-8); [WHO guidance, which does not adopt that cut](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/documents/child-maltreatment/ace-iq-guidance-for-analysing.pdf)
- US adolescent "22.4% with 4 or more": an 8-item YRBS module. — [Swedo et al.](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2024-066633)
- Mexican adolescents, 90% with one or more and about one third with six or more: a 16-adversity adaptation. — [DOI 10.1016/j.chiabu.2023.106492](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2023.106492)
- Chen et al. binary 42.6% and frequency 12.2% at 4 or more: 12 categories, and binary neglect does not follow the survey rule. — [Chen et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42844-022-00076-8)
- Pace community "mean of three" and clinical "mean of 4.93": pooled across studies that often do not state the scoring method. — [Pace et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2022.105640); [Pace et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/car.2883)
- A KIDSCREEN raw sum, including 25 out of 40 or 35 out of 50, is not a T-score. International T for raw 35 was not retrieved. — [evaluation by hand](https://www.kidscreen.org/english/analysis/evaluation-by-hand/); [manual Table A2-16](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/0/module/12416751312/name/KIDSCREEN_manual_En%20glish.pdf)
- Panter-Brick mean 50.30: a different 12-item set. — [Panter-Brick et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12868)
- Kolkata cut of 31 and Tehran mean 27.8: 3-point CYRM-12, total 12–36. — [Kolkata PDF](https://www.ijpediatrics.com/index.php/ijcp/article/download/1738/1287); [Tehran PDF](https://hdq.uswr.ac.ir/article-1-498-en.pdf)

### Inferences
- None of the figures in this section should be printed as a community or clinical benchmark for the JA-MENTOR totals.

### Gaps
