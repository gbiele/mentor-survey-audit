# ACE-IQ distributions and CYRM-12 validation means

Gap-fill, 29 October 2026. This note does not repeat the Malawi, Chilean, Chinese 12-category, Polish clinical, or Turkish school figures already extracted in `ace_kidscreen_cyrm.md`. A percentage is reported only when the opened text states it. Complements were not calculated and then presented as published percents.

# ACE-IQ adolescent or European distributions

## What a second search found for the WHO 13-category totals

### Takeaway
No school or general-population adolescent study was found that reports the percent at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more on the WHO 13-category ACE-IQ, for either the binary total or the frequency total, in Europe or in the other countries searched. The closest new distribution is a 2019 representative sample of Slovak adults, which prints both a binary and a frequency column on 13 domains but pools 2 and 3. German, Hong Kong, and Vietnamese student papers are young-adult samples, and the German total is not the survey's 13 categories.

### Cited Findings
- Slovakia, adults, binary and a frequency column, 13 domains. Representative sample, April 2019, n = 1018, age 18–85, mean 46.2 (SD 16.6), 48.7% men. The paper names the WHO ACE-IQ and lists 13 domains: emotional neglect, physical neglect, parental substance use, parental mental illness, incarcerated household member, parental separation, domestic violence, emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, bullying, community violence, and collective violence. The methods paragraph says binary scoring was the method used in the study: a yes at "once, several times or frequently" counts, total 0–13. The same paragraph describes frequency scoring only as recording how often the event occurred. Table 2 nevertheless has a column headed as frequency scoring. Alpha on this sample was 0.78. — [Kaščáková, Fürstová, Hašto, and Tavel, 2025, Preventive Medicine Reports, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11788736/)
- Slovak binary total, sexes combined: mean 2.7 (SD 2.6). No ACE 22.9%. One ACE 22.0%. Two to three ACEs 23.7%. Four or more 31.4%. Men: mean 2.8 (SD 2.7); no ACE 110/496 (22.2%); one ACE 101 (20.4%); two to three 119 (24.0%); four or more 166 (33.5%). Women: mean 2.5 (SD 2.6); two to three 122 (23.4%); four or more 154 (29.5%). The fetched table text did not preserve the women's counts for zero and for one. Scores of exactly 2 and exactly 3 are not printed. — [Kaščáková et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11788736/)
- Slovak frequency column, sexes combined only: mean 0.9 (SD 1.5). No ACE 547 (53.7%). One ACE 268 (26.3%). Two to three ACEs 128 (12.6%). Four or more 75 (7.4%). These four counts sum to 1018. Yes/no categories are identical in the binary and frequency columns (household substance use 11.1%, incarceration 2.1%, household mental illness 3.9%, parental separation or death 32.5%, collective violence 8.9%). Categories that drop in the frequency column include physical abuse 23.6% to 1.7%, emotional abuse 34.4% to 3.0%, household member treated violently 38.1% to 11.9%, emotional neglect 30.9% to 9.1%, physical neglect 19.0% to 3.1%, bullying 13.5% to 0.9%, and community violence 43.6% to 4.6%. Sexual abuse is 3.7% in the binary column and 1.5% in the frequency column, which is not the WHO rule that any sexual abuse counts in both totals. — [Kaščáková et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11788736/)
- Germany, university students, frequency coding, not the survey's 13 categories. Public-university students, May–June 2017, net n = 1466, mean age 24.09 (SD 4.49), 80.3% female before weighting. The authors started from the ACE-IQ, then added serious financial problems and caregiver absence, combined physical fighting with community violence into one "peer violence" category, and pooled caregiver death with parental absence. The resulting 13 are physical abuse, emotional abuse, physical neglect, emotional neglect, sexual abuse, household substance abuse, household mental illness, domestic violence, parental separation or divorce, parental absence or death, serious financial problems, bullying, and peer violence. Collective violence is not its own category. Items were dichotomised with the WHO frequency-version coding manual. A cumulative score was summed, range 0–13. The opened text does not state the mean of that sum. — [Wiehn, Hornberg, and Fischer, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-018-5926-3); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6090638/)
- Wiehn distribution. The paper says weighting was applied to descriptive percentages and that absolute numbers are unweighted, so the percentages and the counts are not the same metric. Weighted: 73.8% marked positive for any of their 13 ACEs; one ACE 22.9%; two ACEs 15.7%; three ACEs 9.0%; four or more 24.6%. Unweighted counts in the same sentence and in the regression table: one ACE n = 348, two n = 216, three n = 135, four or more n = 355, and the no-exposure reference category n = 412. The opened text does not print a percent at zero. The four printed positive percentages sum to 72.2, not to the printed 73.8% with any ACE, so a zero percent was not derived here. — [Wiehn et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-018-5926-3)
- Hong Kong, young adults, frequency scoring, 13 categories, not adolescents. Associate and bachelor students at two universities and affiliate colleges, April–June 2017, n = 433, age 18–24, mean 20.16 (SD 1.67), 178 men (41.1%). The author manuscript says the frequency method was used and that the analysis covers the full set of 13 ACE-IQ categories. Collective-violence items were kept; the words "soldiers" and "militia" were removed because they were not considered applicable in Hong Kong. At least one ACE: 74.36%. Two or more: 48.18%. The discussion states that 31% reported three or more. The manuscript text that was opened does not state a four-or-more percent, a percent at zero, or a sample mean and SD of the total. Latent-class means (Low ACE 0.83, SD 0.82; Household Violence 3.31, SD 0.94; Multiple ACEs 4.95, SD 1.53) are class means, not the sample mean. — [Ho, Chan, Chien, Bressington, and Karatzias, author manuscript of the 2019 Child Abuse & Neglect paper](https://napier-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1369641); [DOI 10.1016/j.chiabu.2018.11.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2018.11.009)
- Vietnam, university students, scoring method not in the abstract. Eight medical universities, n = 2099. The abstract says the questionnaire was the WHO ACE-IQ, that 76% reported at least one exposure, and that 21% had four or more. It does not give age, a mean, binary versus frequency, or the percent at 0, 1, 2, or 3. The most common adversities in the abstract were emotional abuse 42.3%, physical abuse 39.9%, and witnessing a household member treated violently 34.6%. — [Tran, Dunne, Vo, and Luu, 2015, abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1010539515589812)
- Ho's manuscript, describing Tran rather than quoting Tran's abstract, says Tran used the same adjusted frequency method and that 37% reported three or more. That 37% and the scoring label were not re-read in Tran. Ho also describes Kim (2017), Korean college students n = 939, as about 50% with at least one ACE and 15% with three or more, again on that frequency method. Kim was not opened. — [Ho et al. manuscript](https://napier-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1369641)
- Hungary, school adolescents, wrong instrument. A general community school sample, March 2019–January 2022, n = 792, grades 6–11, age 12–17, used the 10-item ACE questionnaire. The abstract presents it as a measure of intrafamilial adversity. It is not the WHO 13-category ACE-IQ, so no prevalence from it is used here. — [Kovács-Tóth, Oláh, Kuritárné Szabó, and Fekete, 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1161620)
- Searches for a WHO ACE-IQ school or general-population adolescent distribution in Germany, Norway, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, Poland, Spain, and Hungary returned the 10-item Hungarian questionnaire, the Dutch 10-item clinical form already excluded in the earlier note, and the German university study above. They did not return a 13-category adolescent percent at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more. Pace et al. (2024), already cited in the earlier note, still describes European ACE-IQ prevalence data as too sparse to pool. — [Kovács-Tóth et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1161620); [Wiehn et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-018-5926-3)

### Inferences
- A European adolescent distribution on the survey's binary total, and a separate one on the survey's frequency total, is still not available. The Slovak table is the closest European general-population split that uses the 13 named domains and prints both columns. It is an adult distribution. It pools 2 with 3. The binary total is not a confirmed translation onto the survey: the methods text does not say whether "sometimes" counts as binary emotional neglect, and binary emotional neglect (30.9%) is much higher than the frequency column (9.1%). The frequency column is closer to a severity threshold, but sexual abuse changing from 3.7% to 1.5% means that column is not a clean copy of the WHO rule that sexual abuse counts at any occurrence in both totals.
- Wiehn et al. (2018) is the closest German count at 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more, and it uses the WHO frequency manual, but the category list drops collective violence, merges community violence into peer violence, and adds financial problems. It must stay labelled as not the survey's 13-category total. The sample is university students with a mean age of 24, not adolescents. No mean of the sum was found to report.
- Ho et al. is a 13-category frequency score in young adults, with a small wording change on collective violence. It supplies percent at least one (74.36%), percent at two or more (48.18%), and a discussion figure of 31% at three or more. It does not supply the 0 / 1 / 2 / 3 / 4+ split or a sample mean. Tran's abstract supplies 76% at least one and 21% at four or more for Vietnamese medical students, without a scoring method. Neither is an adolescent norm.
- The Hungarian school study is a real adolescent European ACE survey, and it is the 10-item questionnaire. Its figures are not ACE-IQ norms.

### Gaps
- No percent at exactly 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more was found for adolescents on the WHO 13-category binary total or the WHO 13-category frequency total.
- No Norwegian, Dutch, Spanish, Polish, or British school or general-population ACE-IQ distribution on those 13 categories was found. A published WHO pilot-site table with that split was not found either.
- The Slovak frequency column could not be checked item by item against WHO Table 2. The sexual-abuse mismatch is unresolved in the fetched text.
- Tran's full text was not opened, so the scoring method and the 37% at three or more remain Ho's description of Tran. Kim (2017) was not opened.

# CYRM-12 means from the original validation

## Liebenberg 2013, Jefferies 2018, and a European adolescent mean

### Takeaway
Re-reading Liebenberg, Ungar, and LeBlanc (2013) confirms that the original CYRM-12 validation does not report a total-score mean or SD on the 12–60 scale for either the school sample or the multiple-service sample. Jefferies, McGarrigle, and Ungar is the Rasch revision to the 17-item CYRM-R, not a CYRM-12 norms paper, and the sources opened for it give no 12–60 mean. No Norwegian, German, British, Spanish, or Dutch school or general-population adolescent mean on the Liebenberg 12 items scored 12–60 was found. No source opened here sets a standard clinical cutoff for the CYRM-12.

### Cited Findings
- Liebenberg, Ungar, and LeBlanc (2013) reduced the CYRM-28 to 12 items in two Atlantic Canada samples. Multiple-service-using youth (child welfare, mental health, juvenile justice, special education, and community programmes): n = 122, mean age 18. School sample used for the confirmatory analysis: the abstract says n = 1,494, mean age 15. Cronbach's alpha for the 12 items in the school sample was 0.840. The fit statistic is printed as χ² (51, N = 1540). The paper's descriptive table is item means during item reduction, including items that are not in the final 12, such as "I cooperate with people around me." The opened text does not report a summed CYRM-12 mean or SD for either sample. — [Liebenberg, Ungar, and LeBlanc, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf03405676); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6974279/)
- Jefferies, McGarrigle, and Ungar analysed the CYRM with a Rasch model in n = 408 participants in Canada, aged 11–19, from the Pathways to Resilience study. The result they recommend is a 17-item, two-subscale CYRM-R (intra/interpersonal and caregiver). The abstract and the publisher record opened here do not report a mean or SD, on the 12–60 metric or on the 17-item metric. The Resilience Research Centre manual cites this paper as the revision and recommends the 17-item CYRM-R rather than the CYRM-12. — [Jefferies, McGarrigle, and Ungar, Journal of Evidence-Informed Social Work](https://doi.org/10.1080/23761407.2018.1548403); [RRC manual v2.5, 2022](https://centerofinnovationnm.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/CYRM__ARM-User_Manual.pdf.pdf)
- The same manual says the Centre does not provide a score that means "good" or "normal" resilience, because any threshold would vary by context, and that norms are not well established. Appendix C then prints tentative quartile bands from one Canadian study, and says to use them with caution. That sample is 1,596 English-speaking youth aged 11–20 (mean 14.83, SD 1.97, 49.8% female) in Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, and Labrador. The bands assume a 5-point scale whose maximum is 85: the conversion example divides by 85. Low / moderate / high / exceptional cut-points are below 61, 61–69, 70–75, and 76 or above for multiple-service users, and below 63, 63–70, 71–76, and 77 or above for a community comparison group. Those bands are for the 17-item total. The manual does not print a CYRM-12 mean, and it does not set a CYRM-12 clinical cutoff. — [RRC manual v2.5, Appendix C](https://centerofinnovationnm.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/CYRM__ARM-User_Manual.pdf.pdf)
- European adolescents on a scale the authors call the CYRM-12, but not a published 12–60 mean, and not a confirmed item match. RefugeesWellSchool, January 2019–September 2020, n = 1607 immigrant and refugee adolescents in Belgium, Denmark, Finland, Norway, Sweden, and the United Kingdom, mean age 15.3 (SD 2.15), 42.3% girls. The paper cites Liebenberg et al. (2013) and says items were rated 1 = not at all, 2 = a little, 3 = somewhat, 4 = quite a bit, 5 = a lot. The example it prints is "Do you feel you fit in with other peers?", which is not one of the 12 Liebenberg statements. Profile indicators, including resilience, were standardized to the sample mean. The opened text does not report a raw sample mean or SD on the 12–60 sum. — [Aalto, Punamäki, Vänskä, and colleagues, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2025.2479924); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11951332/)
- Nearby European papers that are a different form. A Spanish adaptation in at-risk youth aged 12–18 produced a 32-item scale, not the Liebenberg 12. A Dutch validation is the CYRM-28. Neither mean is a CYRM-12 mean. — [Spanish CYRM-32](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01410); [Dutch CYRM-28](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.637760/full)
- No standard clinical cutoff for the CYRM-12 appears in Liebenberg et al. (2013), in the Jefferies abstract, or in the RRC manual's CYRM-12 material. The manual's only numeric bands are the Appendix C quartiles for the 85-point scale, presented with a warning against hard cut-offs. — [Liebenberg et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf03405676); [Jefferies et al.](https://doi.org/10.1080/23761407.2018.1548403); [RRC manual v2.5](https://centerofinnovationnm.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/CYRM__ARM-User_Manual.pdf.pdf)

### Inferences
- There is still no original-validation mean to put beside a survey score. The school sample (Atlantic Canada, mean age 15, about 1,494–1,540) and the multiple-service sample (n = 122, mean age 18) exist, and their 12-item totals were not published. Item means in the reduction table must not be added up: the table mixes retained and discarded items, and it is not a person-level total.
- Jefferies, McGarrigle, and Ungar did not publish the missing CYRM-12 norm. Their sample (Canada, ages 11–19, n = 408) was used to build the 17-item CYRM-R. A 17-item sum runs to 85 if each item is scored 1–5. Subtracting 12 would not put that sum on the survey's 0–48 metric. No 17-item mean was in the sources opened, so none is reported.
- The survey conversion, if a real 12–60 sum is found later, is that sum minus 12. That step assumes the published "not at all" or "does not describe me at all" is 1 and the survey's corresponding anchor is 0, with the other four anchors in the same order, and that no item is reversed. It was not applied here, because this search did not find a new 12–60 mean.
- The Appendix C numbers (61, 63, 70, 76, 77) are quartile labels on an 85-point Canadian sample. They are not a clinical cutoff, and they are not CYRM-12 scores. Dividing them by 85 and multiplying by 60 would be a rescaling the manual allows for a shortened form, not a published CYRM-12 norm. That rescaling was not done.
- Aalto et al. (2025) is the European adolescent study this search found that names the CYRM-12 and includes Norway and the United Kingdom. It cannot be used as the survey benchmark: the sample is immigrant and refugee youth rather than a general school population, the printed example item is not a Liebenberg item, and the results are standardized profile scores rather than a 12–60 mean.
- There is no standard clinical cutoff for the CYRM-12 in the sources opened.

### Gaps
- A mean and SD on the 12–60 metric from Liebenberg et al. (2013) does not exist in the paper. The Jefferies paper's full PDF was not retrieved beyond the abstract, the DOI record, and the manual's citation of it; those do not contain a 12–60 mean.
- No general-population or ordinary school mean for adolescents in Norway, Germany, the United Kingdom, Spain, or the Netherlands was found on the Liebenberg 12 items scored 1–5.
- Aalto et al. may have used the standard 12 items and only worded the example poorly. The item list was not in the text that was opened, so the match could not be confirmed, and there was still no raw mean to convert.
