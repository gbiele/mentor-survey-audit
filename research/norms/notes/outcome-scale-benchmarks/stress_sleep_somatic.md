# Stress, sleep, somatic, child-anxiety, procrastination, and ACT scale benchmarks

Notes for judging whether a reported score looks like a non-clinical population or a clinical one. Figures below were read from the cited papers or official scoring guides. Where a number could not be recovered, it is listed under Gaps and is not estimated.

Catalogue context used only to know which score a reader will see: GetStarted (PSS-10, IPS; Dutch university students), I-Sleep (ISI; Dutch general practice), Tankevirus (SSS-8; Norway; write-up says 58% high or very high), Cool Minds (SCAS and CAS-8; Denmark, about 7–17), Bene in Mente (AAQ-II, VLQ; Italian university students), Young Spotlight (PSAS ≥55; Norway, ages 13–16).

## 1. PSS-10 (Perceived Stress Scale, 10-item)

### Takeaway
The PSS-10 is a 0–40 stress total with US probability-sample means mostly in the mid-teens for adults under 55, and around 11 from age 65. Cohen published no diagnostic cutoff. Bands such as 0–13 / 14–26 / 27–40 are an unofficial applied convention. European university students, including a large Dutch survey, average about 20.

### Cited Findings
- Full name: 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10). Ten items, each 0 (never) to 4 (very often), four positively worded items reverse-scored, sum 0–40. Higher = more perceived stress over the last month (unpredictable, uncontrollable, overloaded). The original Cohen, Kamarck, and Mermelstein instrument was 14 items; Cohen and Williamson recommended the 10-item form. — [Cohen and Janicki-Deverts 2012 PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/stress-immunity-disease-lab/publications/scalesmeasurements/pdfs/whos_stressed_jasp_2012.pdf); [Denovan et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/03075079.2017.1340445)
- Cohen did not publish diagnostic cutoffs or severity bands. The 2012 norm paper treats the PSS-10 as a continuous score and reports means, not case thresholds. — [Cohen and Janicki-Deverts 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1559-1816.2012.00900.x)
- Unadjusted US means, 2009 eNation survey (the table most often cited as current US norms). Age: under 25, n=223, M=16.78, SD=6.86; 25–34, n=433, M=17.46, SD=7.31; 35–44, n=331, M=16.38, SD=7.07; 45–54, n=419, M=16.94, SD=7.83; 55–64, n=372, M=14.50, SD=7.20; 65 and older, n=222, M=11.09, SD=6.77. Sex, 2009: men n=968, M=15.52, SD=7.44; women n=1032, M=16.14, SD=7.56. — [Cohen and Janicki-Deverts 2012 PDF, Table 1](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/stress-immunity-disease-lab/publications/scalesmeasurements/pdfs/whos_stressed_jasp_2012.pdf)
- The same table’s 1983 Harris Poll means (the earlier national sample) are lower. Age under 25: n=349, M=14.54, SD=5.95; 25–34: M=13.45, SD=6.45; 65 and older: M=11.98, SD=6.31. Sex, 1983: men M=12.07, SD=5.89; women M=13.68, SD=6.57. 2006 age means were higher than 2009 for the youngest adults (under 25: M=18.64, SD=7.24). — [same Table 1](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/stress-immunity-disease-lab/publications/scalesmeasurements/pdfs/whos_stressed_jasp_2012.pdf)
- The paper does not print one overall mean collapsed across age. Internal consistency was α=.78 in the 1983 sample and α=.91 in 2006 and 2009. — [Cohen and Janicki-Deverts 2012 PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/stress-immunity-disease-lab/publications/scalesmeasurements/pdfs/whos_stressed_jasp_2012.pdf)
- UK university students (N=524 social-science students at one post-1992 university): PSS-10 M=19.79, SD=6.37. Women M=20.19, SD=6.03; men M=19.25, SD=6.76; the gender difference was not significant. The authors describe this as higher than a general-population comparison they cite for ages 18–29 (M=14.2, SD=6.2, attributed to Cohen 1994). — [Denovan, Dagnall, Dhingra, and Grogan 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/03075079.2017.1340445)
- Dutch university students, Caring Universities sixth survey, 2025, n=11,167: PSS-10 M=20.10, SD=6.8. Using bands they call standardized categories — low 0–13 (18.4%), moderate 14–26 (63.1%), severe 27–40 (18.5%) — 18.5% were in the top band. This is a student wellbeing survey, not a clinical sample and not a Cohen norm table. — [Caring Universities sixth survey report 2025, PDF](https://moodlift.nl/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Caring-Universities-sixth-survey-report-2025.pdf)
- Those 0–13 / 14–26 / 27–40 bands also appear in other applied papers (for example a European student survey that defines 0–13 low, 14–26 moderate, 27–40 high). They are not in the Cohen and Janicki-Deverts norm paper. — [BMC Public Health European student survey](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12889-025-25001-3); contradicted as official cutoffs by the absence of any cutoff in [Cohen and Janicki-Deverts 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1559-1816.2012.00900.x)

### Inferences
- A GetStarted score near 16–18 matches US adults under 45 in 2009. A score near 20 matches recent UK and Dutch student surveys, which sit above those US adult norms. A score near 27 is in the unofficial “severe” band and is above 1 SD from the US under-25 mean (16.78+6.86≈23.6), but it is not a diagnosis.
- There is no PSS-10 clinical-population mean in the norm papers, because the scale was not built as a case-finder. There is no standard minimal important change in the sources read here.

### Gaps
- No single published overall 2009 mean (only subgroup rows). Not computed here.
- The origin paper of the 0–13 / 14–26 / 27–40 bands was not found. They are widely copied and are not Cohen’s.
- No minimal important change was found.

## 2. Insomnia Severity Index (ISI)

### Takeaway
Seven items, total 0–28. The standard bands are 0–7 none, 8–14 subthreshold, 15–21 moderate, 22–28 severe. Those bands started as guidelines. Later empirical cutoffs differ by setting: about 10 in the community, about 11 for clinical-trial enrollment, and 14 in primary care. A drop of about 8 points matches “moderate improvement,” not the smallest change Morin anchored. I-Sleep’s rule (drop of at least 8, or score below 11) tracks the moderate-improvement anchor and the clinical-trial threshold, not remission into the 0–7 band.

### Cited Findings
- Full name: Insomnia Severity Index. Seven items, each 0–4, total 0–28. Higher = worse insomnia over the past month (onset, maintenance, early awakening, dissatisfaction, interference, noticeability, distress). Patient, clinician, and significant-other forms exist; norms below are the patient form. — [Morin, Belleville, Bélanger, and Ivers 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079939/)
- Standard bands, restated in that paper: absence of insomnia 0–7; subthreshold 8–14; moderate 15–21; severe 22–28. Morin et al. also describe the original classification used in their validity check as <8 no insomnia, 8–14 subthreshold, >14 moderate to severe. A sleep-scale handbook notes that the developers presented these bands as guidelines and said the chosen cutoffs had not been validated at the time. — [Morin et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/34.5.601); [Insomnia Severity Index chapter, University of Pennsylvania CBT-I materials](https://www.med.upenn.edu/cbti/assets/user-content/documents/Insomnia%20Severity%20Index%20(ISI).pdf)
- Community case-finding, Québec epidemiological sample (N=959, mean age 43.8, 24.7% with an insomnia complaint): cutoff of 10 was optimal, sensitivity 86.1%, specificity 87.7%. Morin et al. say a cutoff below 8 yields too many false positives and a cutoff above 14 too many false negatives, and that 10 is the best compromise in a population sample. For enrolling patients in clinical trials they say 15 is too strict and 11 is the best compromise. — [Morin et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079939/)
- Treatment response in the clinical sample (183 people with an insomnia diagnosis plus 62 controls without insomnia). Mean ISI change by independent-assessor rating: slight improvement −4.65 (n=20); moderate improvement −8.36 (95% CI −7.20 to −9.53, n=63); marked improvement −9.89 (95% CI −8.74 to −11.04, n=63). The abstract rounds the moderate-improvement anchor to −8.4 (95% CI −7.1 to −9.4). An ROC analysis found that a reduction greater than 7 points best separated moderate or marked improvement from slight improvement. — [Morin et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079939/)
- The extracted table’s confidence interval for the slight-improvement row was disordered (“2.61 to −6.69”). Only the point estimate −4.65 is used here.
- Primary care, not a sleep clinic. Gagnon, Bélanger, Ivers, and Morin, 410 adults in six Québec general-practice waiting rooms: mean ISI 8.27 (SD 6.44), which the authors place in mild/subthreshold insomnia. The interviewed subsample (n=101) had mean 11.15 (SD 6.76); 34 of 101 (33.7%) met insomnia-disorder criteria. ROC: cutoff of 14 was optimal (sensitivity 82.4%, specificity 82.1%, 82.2% correctly classified, AUC 0.87). A cutoff of 8 detected every interviewed case (sensitivity 100%) but specificity was only 52.2%. — [Gagnon et al. 2013, Journal of the American Board of Family Medicine](https://www.jabfm.org/content/26/6/701)
- I-Sleep’s written rule, as given in the assignment: clinical improvement = ISI change of at least 8 points, or an ISI score below 11. A drop of at least 8 matches Morin’s moderate-improvement mean (about 8.4) and sits just above the >7 ROC cut. “Below 11” matches Morin’s suggested clinical-trial enrollment threshold of 11 (scores of 11 and above were the compromise for counting someone as a case in a trial). A score below 11 can still sit in the subthreshold band (8–10). It is not the same as remission into the 0–7 band. It is also a stricter endpoint than “no longer at or above the primary-care screening cutoff of 14,” because 11, 12, and 13 are below 14 but are not below 11. — comparison of [Morin et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079939/) and [Gagnon et al. 2013](https://www.jabfm.org/content/26/6/701)

### Inferences
- A general-practice attender who has not been selected for insomnia looks like a score around 8 (subthreshold band), not like 0–7 and not like 15–21. A score of 15 or more is the moderate clinical band on the original guide and is at or above the primary-care screening cutoff of 14.
- “Score below 11” means the person has left Morin’s clinical-trial case range. They can still be in the subthreshold band (8–10). “Score below 8” is the stricter remission definition that lands in the original no-insomnia band. A 6-point drop would sit between Morin’s slight anchor (about 4.7) and the >7 / about-8 moderate-improvement standard. This pass did not retrieve a primary paper that defines response as exactly 6 points.

### Gaps
- Group means for Morin 2011’s community sample, the 183 insomnia patients, and the 62 good-sleeper controls were not in the text extracted from the paper. Not invented.
- The ISI mean among the 34 primary-care patients who met insomnia-disorder criteria was not extracted (only the mixed-sample means 8.27 and 11.15).
- Bastien, Vallières, and Morin 2001 (Sleep Medicine), the usual citation for the original bands, was not opened. The bands are quoted from Morin 2011, which reprints them.
- No separate consensus statement was found that remission must be <8 versus <10. Both thresholds are in use because they correspond to different Morin cutoffs (the 0–7 band versus the community case cutoff of 10).

## 3. SSS-8 (Somatic Symptom Scale-8)

### Takeaway
Eight items, total 0–32. Gierk et al. 2014 set five percentile bands in a German general population whose mean was only 3.23: minimal 0–3, low 4–7, medium 8–11, high 12–15, very high 16–32. High or very high was built to be about the top 5%. A Hamburg psychosomatic-outpatient sample averaged 12.96, the bottom of the high band. There is no minimal important change. Tankevirus’s “58% high or very high” is far above the German population rate.

### Cited Findings
- Full name: Somatic Symptom Scale-8. Eight items (gastrointestinal, pain, cardiopulmonary, fatigue), each 0 (not at all) to 4 (very much), past seven days, total 0–32. Higher = greater somatic symptom burden. It is a short form of the PHQ-15. It does not assume a cause and is not itself a somatic-symptom-disorder diagnosis. — [Gierk, Kohlmann, Kroenke, Spangenberg, et al. 2014, JAMA Internal Medicine](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/1783305)
- German general population, June–July 2012, N=2,510, age >13: mean 3.23 (SD 3.96), observed range 0–26. Women 3.49 vs men 2.94. Cronbach α=0.81. Age correlated r=0.32 with the total. — [Gierk et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2013.12179)
- Severity categories were cut from percentile ranks, not from a diagnostic interview. No to minimal 0–3; low 4–7; medium 8–11; high 12–15 (about the 95th to 98th percentile); very high 16–32 (≥98th percentile). The authors’ shorthand: scores of 4, 8, 12, and 16 mark low, medium, high, and very high. They chose the top two bands to match an expectation that high or very high burden is less common in the general population than the roughly 10% they cite for primary care. Each step up in category was associated with a 53% increase in health-care visits (95% CI 44% to 63%). — [Gierk et al. 2014](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/1783305)
- The same paper says a minimal clinically important difference could not be estimated: the study was cross-sectional, and longitudinal data would be required. No MCID is given. — [Gierk et al. 2014](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/1783305)
- German psychosomatic outpatients, N=131, mean age 41.87 (SD 15.03), 66% women: SSS-8 mean 12.96 (SD 6.50). PHQ-15 in the same people was 12.12 (SD 5.63); the two totals correlated r=0.83. α for the SSS-8 was 0.76. The authors say that, in this sample, PHQ-15-style thresholds (0–4, 5–9, 10–14, 15–32) grouped patients more like the PHQ-15 than the 2014 SSS-8 bands did, and they still recommend the previously established (2014) thresholds until more populations are studied. — [Gierk et al. 2015, Journal of Psychosomatic Research](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychores.2014.11.006)
- A later German representative sample (not the 2014 survey), N=2,515: mean 4.64 (SD 4.86). Women 5.14 (SD 5.03), men 4.10 (SD 4.61). Category shares using the 2014 bands: no to minimal 53.8%, low 24.6%, medium 11.8%, high 5.6%, very high 4.3%. A score of 12 was about the 92nd percentile in this newer sample, not the 95th. — [Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2026, German community norms](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1851921/full)

### Inferences
- A score under 4 is the typical German general-population range (2014 mean 3.23; more than half of the 2026 sample were in 0–3). A score of 12 or more is “high or very high” on the standard bands and was about the top 5% by the 2014 design, or about 10% (5.6%+4.3%) in the 2026 German community sample. The Hamburg outpatient mean, 12.96, sits on the lower edge of “high,” not in “very high.”
- If 58% of Tankevirus users are high or very high (score ≥12), that share is several times the German population rate. It resembles a selected, symptom-heavy group more than a community survey. That comparison uses the user’s statement of the 58% figure; the Tankevirus paper itself was not re-read here.

### Gaps
- No Norwegian general-population SSS-8 mean was retrieved.
- No minimal important change exists in the 2014 paper, and none was found later in this pass.

## 4. Spence Children’s Anxiety Scale (SCAS), child self-report

### Takeaway
The child form has 44 items, of which 38 are scored, total 0–114. Norms are published by age and gender, not as one cutoff. The developer’s rule of thumb is a T-score: under 60 is within the normal range, 60 or above is “elevated,” and the scale is not a diagnosis. Danish school means are lower than the Australian website means, and Danish clinical thresholds from the CEBU scoring guide are age- and sex-specific numbers around 22–36, not a single cutoff.

### Cited Findings
- Child form: 44 items, 38 anxiety items scored, 6 positive fillers not scored (items 11, 17, 26, 31, 38, 43). Each scored item 0 (never) to 3 (always). Total 0–114. Higher = more anxiety symptoms. Subscales: panic/agoraphobia 0–27, generalized anxiety 0–18, social phobia 0–18, physical-injury fears 0–15, obsessive-compulsive 0–18, separation anxiety 0–18. The developer site says the scale is often used from about 7 through 17, with the 8–11 T-score table used at age 7 and the 12–15 table used at 16–17. The Danish scoring guide says the self-report was built for ages 8–15 and is also used at 7 and up to 18. — [CEBU Danish scoring guide, November 2024](https://psy.au.dk/fileadmin/PSY/Centre/CEBU/Scoringsredskaber/Opgoerelse_og_scoring_af_SCAS__ny_dansk_version__nov2024.pdf); [SCAS scoring and interpretation](https://www.scaswebsite.com/portfolio/scas-child-scoring-and-interpretation/)
- T-score convention on the developer site: community scores are rescaled to mean 50, SD 10. T below 60 (below about the 85th percentile) is called the normal range. T of 60 or more (about the top 15%) is “elevated,” not automatically clinical. T of 65 is about the top 6%; some clinicians use 65 as a clinical flag (about 1.5 SD). T of 70 is about the top 2%. The site says the SCAS is not a diagnostic instrument and should be read with age and gender tables. — [SCAS T-score page](https://www.scaswebsite.com/portfolio/scas-child-t-scores/); [scoring page](https://www.scaswebsite.com/portfolio/scas-child-scoring-and-interpretation/)
- Raw totals that sit at T=50 (50th percentile) on the June 2018 developer tables, which are percentile maps, not means: boys 8–11, total 23–24; girls 8–11, total 31–32; girls 12–15, total 25. Elevated (T=60) starts at about total 40–41 for boys 8–11, 50–51 for girls 8–11, and 40–42 for girls 12–15. — [Boys 8–11 table](https://www.scaswebsite.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/SCAS-Boys-8-11-FinalJune2018-1.pdf); [Girls 8–11 table](https://www.scaswebsite.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/SCAS-Girls-8-11-FinalJune2018.pdf); [Girls 12–15 table](https://www.scaswebsite.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/SCAS-Girls-12-15-FinalJune2018.pdf)
- Reardon et al. 2018 quote website norms for the age band comparable to their 7–11 sample as means, which sit a little above those 50th-percentile raw scores: boys, child-report mean 26.65 (parent 16.0); girls, child-report mean 34.02 (parent 15.9). Their own English community means were close for boys (child 26.12) and a bit higher for girls (child 36.18). — [Reardon et al. 2018, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6179143/)
- Danish validation cited by the intervention text and by the CEBU guide: Arendt, Hougaard, and Thastum, Journal of Anxiety Disorders, 2014, community and clinical samples, ages covered in the guide as 7–17. DOI 10.1016/j.janxdis.2014.09.021. — [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0887618514001455)
- Child self-report means in the November 2024 CEBU guide. The guide attributes school-child norms to Arendt, Hougaard, and Thastum 2014, and clinical norms to Danish children with an anxiety diagnosis seen in CEBU Cool Kids/Chilled and Cool Kids for social phobia (so the clinical rows are not claimed here to be a verbatim reprint of one table in the 2014 article). School, girls 7–12: total 27.74 (SD 15.10), n=315. Girls 13–17: 26.23 (12.85), n=168. Boys 7–12: 18.44 (13.36), n=312. Boys 13–17: 13.82 (9.17), n=177. Clinical anxiety diagnosis, girls 7–12: 36.49 (11.88), n=99. Girls 13–17: 49.58 (17.27), n=118. Boys 7–12: 34.42 (16.97), n=95. Boys 13–17: 35.68 (16.57), n=38. — [CEBU guide](https://psy.au.dk/fileadmin/PSY/Centre/CEBU/Scoringsredskaber/Opgoerelse_og_scoring_af_SCAS__ny_dansk_version__nov2024.pdf)
- Danish clinical thresholds in that guide use Jacobson and Truax method C (the point where the child looks more like the clinical group than the school group). They are proposed Danish cutoffs, not the developer’s T=60 rule. Child total: girls 7–12, cutoff 32.64, reliable change 11.84; girls 13–17, cutoff 36.19, reliable change 10.07; boys 7–12, cutoff 25.48, reliable change 10.47; boys 13–17, cutoff 21.61, reliable change 7.19. The guide’s worked example: a 12-year-old girl above 32 looks more like the anxiety group; a later drop of more than about 10 points, to below the cutoff, is both clinically and statistically meaningful on their rule. — [CEBU guide](https://psy.au.dk/fileadmin/PSY/Centre/CEBU/Scoringsredskaber/Opgoerelse_og_scoring_af_SCAS__ny_dansk_version__nov2024.pdf)

### Inferences
- For Cool Minds (Danish, roughly 7–17, child report), the CEBU age-and-sex means and method-C cutoffs are the relevant benchmark. A girl of 7–12 around 28 is a Danish school mean; around 33 she crosses the Danish clinical threshold; the Danish clinical mean at that age is about 36. A boy of 13–17 around 14 is a Danish school mean; the Danish clinical threshold is about 22 and the clinical mean about 36.
- Australian website means quoted by Reardon (boys 26.65, girls 34.02) are higher than Danish school means (boys 7–12: 18.44; girls 7–12: 27.74). The same raw score can be ordinary on the Australian tables and high on the Danish school norms. The developer T-score and the Danish method-C cutoff are different rules and will not flag the same children.

### Gaps
- Boys 12–15 T-score table was not opened, so the Australian 50th-percentile raw total for that cell is missing.
- The 1998 Spence article’s own norm table was not re-extracted. Website tables and Reardon’s quotation of the website are the Australian figures used here.
- The 2014 journal PDF was not opened, so it is not confirmed whether every clinical mean in the 2024 CEBU guide matches the printed 2014 table. The guide itself says clinical figures also come from later CEBU treatment samples.
- Father-report rows in the PDF extract were jumbled and are omitted. Mother-report totals are in the same guide if a parent score is needed later.

## 5. CAS-8 / 8-item child SCAS (SCAS-C-8)

### Takeaway
Cool Minds’ “CAS-8” matches the published 8-item child short form of the SCAS (SCAS-C-8; Reardon et al. 2018), not a separately normed scale under the name CAS-8. Range is 0–24. In English children aged 7–11 the community mean was about 6 and the anxiety-clinic mean about 9. The proposed cutoff is 6.5 overall (5.5 boys, 7.5 girls), from that one study, and child-report accuracy was only moderate. Norms do not cover Danish teenagers.

### Cited Findings
- Cool Minds describes the weekly scale as “the 8-item Children’s Anxiety Scale (CAS-8),” “a short version of the SCAS containing 8 items,” with acceptable reliability. — intervention text in `data/Cool_Minds/description.md` (local catalogue, not a psychometric paper)
- The published short form is the SCAS-C-8: eight SCAS child items, each 0–3, developed by Reardon, Spence, Hesse, Shakir, and Creswell, Psychological Assessment, 2018. DOI 10.1037/pas0000570. Obsessive-compulsive items were left out. The developer site points users to that paper for means and cutoffs and does not host its own scoring manual for the short form. — [SCAS 8-item page](https://www.scaswebsite.com/portfolio/scas-8-item-short-form/); [Reardon et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6179143/)
- Samples: English community children n=361 and clinic-referred children with a primary anxiety disorder n=338, both aged 7–11 (community mean age 9.50, SD 1.09; clinic mean age 9.70, SD 1.36). — [Reardon et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6179143/)
- Child-report SCAS-C-8 means (Table 5). Total: community 5.97 (SD 4.70), clinic 9.18 (SD 4.67), d=0.69. Boys: community 4.90 (4.47), clinic 8.44 (4.70), d=0.77. Girls: community 6.84 (4.71), clinic 9.93 (4.53), d=0.67. Full-length child SCAS in the same table, for scale: community 31.68 (21.02), clinic 39.76 (18.47). — [Reardon et al. 2018, Table 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6179143/)
- Proposed cutoffs, one study, child report: 6.5 in the total sample, sensitivity .67, specificity .64 (they could not reach sensitivity above .70 with specificity above .60). Boys 5.5 (sensitivity .73, specificity .70). Girls 7.5 (sensitivity .64, specificity .63). AUC was above .70. Parent-report SCAS-P-8 discriminated better (cutoff 7.5, sensitivity .85, specificity .75, AUC .86). The authors say parent report should be prioritized for identification. — [Reardon et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6179143/)
- Eight items scored 0–3 give a theoretical total of 0–24. The paper’s means (about 6 and 9) and cutoffs (5.5 to 7.5) sit inside that range. The article text extracted here states the item count and the 0–3 SCAS response format; it does not print the phrase “range 0–24.”

### Inferences
- A weekly CAS-8 around 6 is the English community mean for ages 7–11 and is also the total-sample cutoff, which is why sensitivity and specificity are modest. A score around 9 matches the anxiety-clinic mean in that study. These are not Danish norms and they stop at age 11, while Cool Minds extends toward 17.
- Calling the scale CAS-8 in the catalogue does not by itself prove the item set is identical to SCAS-C-8. The description (8-item SCAS short form) points to Reardon, and no other published “CAS-8” with its own norms turned up in this search.

### Gaps
- No Danish CAS-8 / SCAS-C-8 means or cutoff were found.
- No norms for ages 12–17.
- Item-level identity between Cool Minds’ CAS-8 and Reardon’s eight items was not checked against a Cool Minds item list.
- No minimal important change for the short form was found.

## 6. Irrational Procrastination Scale (IPS)

### Takeaway
Nine items, rated 1–5, three reverse-scored. Published means are usually the item mean (about 2.7–3.6), not the total. A total runs from 9 to 45. A printed 3.2 is an item mean and is ordinary; a printed 32 is a total and is above the middle of Steel’s feedback sheet. There is no clinical cutoff. Steel’s bands are percentiles from a self-selected web sample.

### Cited Findings
- The IPS (Steel, 2010) has nine items. Three are reverse-scored (items 2, 6, and 9 in the 2010 appendix: getting to important tasks before lesser ones, spending time wisely, doing things when one believes they need to be done). Responses are 1 (very seldom or not true of me) to 5 (very often true or true of me). Higher, after reversal = more irrational delay. — [Steel 2010 appendix PDF](https://procrastinus.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/arousal-avoidant-and-decisional-procrastinators-do-they-exist.pdf); [Svartdal et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5005418/)
- A public “Procrastination Quotient” handout uses the same nine-item, 1–5 format but a different item order, and therefore reverse-scores questions 2, 5, and 8. It asks for a total score and prints percentile bands, not a diagnosis: 19 or less, bottom 10%; 20–23, bottom 10–25%; 24–31, middle 50%; 32–36, top 10–25%; 37 or more, top 10%. Those bands describe “everyone else” on that handout. They are not a clinical cutoff. — [IPS handout, procrastinus.com](https://procrastinus.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/irrational-procrastination-scale.pdf)
- Steel and Ferrari 2013, English-speaking web sample, N=16,413, mean age 38.3 (SD 14): IPS item mean 3.57 (SD 0.85), α=.91. People completed the scale on a site that gave them feedback on their procrastination, so the sample is self-selected. Steel’s summary of that study gives sex means of 3.65 for men and 3.5 for women, on the same 1–5 item metric. — [Steel and Ferrari 2013 PDF](https://procrastinus.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/sex-education-and-procrastination.pdf); [About the measure, procrastinus.com](https://procrastinus.com/piers-steel/about-the-measure/)
- Six European countries, students and employees, N=2,893 (Finland, Germany, Italy, Norway, Poland, Sweden). IPS scored as an item mean on the 1–5 scale. Sweden 2.91 (SD 0.80); Finland 2.99 (0.79); Norway 2.99 (0.79); Germany 2.79 (0.78); Italy 2.68 (0.65); Poland 3.22 (0.81). Men scored higher than women; the two sex means were 2.91 and 3.04 (the sentence does not lock which mean is which sex). These country rows mix students and employees. — [Svartdal, Steel, and colleagues 2016, Table 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5005418/)
- Dutch university students, Caring Universities 2025, n=11,167: IPS M=28.86, SD=7.9. They split “no procrastination” (<32, 59.5%) from “problematic procrastination” (>31, 40.5%). 28.86 is a total (9 × about 3.2), not an item mean. The >31 cut matches the handout’s step out of the middle 50% (32 and above), not a diagnostic threshold. — [Caring Universities sixth survey report 2025](https://moodlift.nl/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Caring-Universities-sixth-survey-report-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- Read the metric before comparing. Item mean 3.2 is typical of European student/employee samples (country means about 2.7–3.2) and is below the self-selected web mean of 3.57. Total 32 is 32/9 ≈ 3.56 as an item mean, which matches that web average and is just into the handout’s upper quarter. Total 28.9, the Dutch student mean, is an item mean of about 3.21.
- No source found here treats a score as a clinical diagnosis. Procrastination is handled as a continuous trait with percentile feedback.

### Gaps
- No IPS mean from a treatment-seeking clinical sample was retrieved in this pass (Rozental and others are cited by Svartdal as having used the scale in treatment-seeking groups, but those means were not opened).
- Student-only versus employee-only IPS means for the six-country study were not extracted; Table 4 is by country.
- No minimal important change was found.
- The sex pairing of 2.91 versus 3.04 is ambiguous in the extracted sentence and is not assigned.

## 7. AAQ-II (Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-II)

### Takeaway
Seven items, total 7–49, higher = more psychological inflexibility. Nonclinical groups in Bond et al. cluster around 17–21; a substance-misuse treatment sample averaged 28.34. Bond’s 24–28 range is a preliminary, indirect flag of distress, and Bond says the scale was not built to diagnose. Later work both moves that student cutoff toward 28–32 and argues that the AAQ-II overlaps with general distress, so a single cutoff is disputed.

### Cited Findings
- AAQ-II, final form: 7 items, each 1 (never true) to 7 (always true), total 7–49. Higher = greater psychological inflexibility / experiential avoidance. A 10-item trial version exists; Bond et al. say the 7- and 10-item versions correlate about r=.96, and the 7-item form is the one they recommend. Mean α across their samples .84 (range .78–.88). 3-month and 12-month retest .81 and .79. — [Bond, Hayes, Baer, Carpenter, et al. 2011, accepted manuscript](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/5531/2/AAQ-II_psychometrics_paper_%28accepted_version%29.pdf); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22035996/); [DOI 10.1016/j.beth.2011.03.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beth.2011.03.007)
- Sample means from that manuscript. Sample 1, University of Nevada students, n=206, the 7-item scale: M=21.41, SD=7.97 (the 10-item figure in the same table is 30.69, SD=9.91; do not mix them). Sample 2, University of Kentucky undergraduates, n=433: M=17.34, SD=4.37. Sample 3, outpatient substance-misuse treatment, New York, n=290: M=28.34, SD=9.92. Sample 4, UK retail-bank employees, n=583: M=18.53, SD=7.52. People not in substance-misuse treatment, pooled: M=18.51, SD=7.05, compared with 28.34 in the treatment sample. — [Bond et al. 2011 manuscript](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/5531/2/AAQ-II_psychometrics_paper_%28accepted_version%29.pdf)
- Bond et al. say the AAQ-II was not designed to diagnose mental disorders and was not built to have a cutoff. They regressed other instruments’ distress cutoffs onto the AAQ-II and got a band of 24–28: BDI-II cutoff 14 predicted AAQ-II 28; GHQ-12 cutoff 4 predicted 24 or 25; SCL-90-R GSI cutoff 0.6 predicted 28. They call scores in that range or above a “limited and preliminary” sign of clinically relevant distress, and they note that 24–28 falls between the nonclinical mean 18.51 and the substance-treatment mean 28.34. — [Bond et al. 2011 manuscript, Table 6](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/5531/2/AAQ-II_psychometrics_paper_%28accepted_version%29.pdf)
- A later US student item-response study (Ong, Pierce, Woods, Twohig, and Levin, 2018) says Bond’s regression method is a weak way to set a cutoff. In their student sample, ROC cutoffs of 28 to 32 best separated students with and without elevated CCAPS-34 distress (28 favored sensitivity, 32 favored specificity). They say those cutoffs should be used tentatively because the sample was homogeneous and classification was not a clinical diagnosis. — [Ong et al. 2018, Utah State open copy](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2764&context=psych_facpub)
- Discriminant-validity criticism. Wolgast (2014) found AAQ-II items loaded with items written to measure distress more strongly than with items written to measure acceptance without functional outcomes. — [Wolgast 2014, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25311291/); [DOI 10.1016/j.beth.2014.07.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beth.2014.07.002)
- Tyndall, Waldeck, Pancani, Whelan, Roche, and Dawson (Journal of Contextual Behavioral Science, DOI 10.1016/j.jcbs.2018.09.005), online sample n=524: the AAQ-II correlated more strongly with DASS-21 depression, anxiety, and stress than the Brief Experiential Avoidance Questionnaire did. They conclude the AAQ-II may be measuring distress, the outcome, rather than the process of inflexibility, and that another inflexibility measure may be needed for ACT outcome research. — [Tyndall et al., author manuscript](https://eprints.chi.ac.uk/id/eprint/3831/1/Accepted%20AAQ-II%20paper%20JCBS.pdf)

### Inferences
- For Italian university students (Bene in Mente), the closest Bond means are student samples around 17–21, not the substance-treatment mean of 28. A score in the mid-20s is where Bond’s preliminary distress band starts. A score around 28 matches both that band’s top end and the substance-treatment mean, and it is also the low end of Ong’s later student ROC range. Because of the Wolgast and Tyndall critiques, that score should be read as “looks distressed,” not as a validated inflexibility diagnosis.
- No official consensus cutoff exists. 24–28 and 28–32 are study-specific proposals, and the scale authors dispute the idea of a diagnostic cut.

### Gaps
- No Italian student mean was retrieved.
- Sample 5 and Sample 6 means from Bond were not extracted (Sample 6 is another US student group).
- No minimal important change was found.

## 8. Valued Living Questionnaire (VLQ)

### Takeaway
Two 10-domain parts, importance and consistency, each rated 1–10. The composite is the mean of the ten products (importance × consistency). Undergraduate composites in the Wilson manuscript were about 60–64. There is no normal range and no clinical cutoff. Higher means more valued action, the opposite direction from the symptom scales above.

### Cited Findings
- The VLQ asks about 10 life domains twice: how important each domain is, and how consistent the person’s actions in the past week were with that value. Each rating is on a 1–10 scale. The working manual says the valued-living composite is the mean of the ten products (importance × consistency for each domain). The manual states that this composite can range from 10 to 100. An earlier scoring approach used discrepancy (consistency minus importance); the manual says to use the composite for assessment and to keep discrepancies for clinical discussion only. — [VLQ working manual, v. 1-15-08](https://contextualscience.org/files/VLQ%20working%20manual%20v1-15-08.pdf)
- The psychometric manuscript (Wilson, Sandoz, Kitchens, and Roberts; written as under review at The Psychological Record) defines the same composite: for each domain, importance × consistency, then the mean of those products. Study 1, N=57, mean age 22.6, two administrations: importance sums 84.65 (SD 10.38) and 84.39 (SD 11.80); consistency sums 68.11 (SD 12.82) and 72.63 (SD 11.93); composites 59.52 (SD 14.14) and 63.68 (SD 15.02). Observed composites ran from 32.5 to 95.10 and from 34.90 to 98.00. Composite internal consistency α=.65 and .74. — [Wilson, Sandoz, Kitchens, and Roberts manuscript PDF](https://contextualscience.org/sites/default/files/VLQ%20manuscript%20Wilson%20Sandoz%20et%20al.pdf)
- Study 2 in that manuscript, undergraduates, mean age 20, 92.3% aged 18–22: importance sum 87.23 (SD 8.69); consistency sum 71.67 (SD 14.37); composite 64.21 (SD 15.41), range 29.40 to 100. — [same manuscript](https://contextualscience.org/sites/default/files/VLQ%20manuscript%20Wilson%20Sandoz%20et%20al.pdf)
- The manuscript header says it was under review at The Psychological Record. The published citation that matches those authors and that title is Wilson, Sandoz, Kitchens, and Roberts, The Psychological Record, 2010. The numbers above were read from the ACBS manuscript PDF, not from the typeset journal pages. — [manuscript PDF](https://contextualscience.org/sites/default/files/VLQ%20manuscript%20Wilson%20Sandoz%20et%20al.pdf)
- Neither the manual nor the manuscript gives a normal cutoff or a clinical cutoff. The manual treats extreme importance, consistency, and discrepancy patterns as clinical hypotheses, not as score thresholds. — [VLQ working manual](https://contextualscience.org/files/VLQ%20working%20manual%20v1-15-08.pdf)
- A separate ACT self-help scoring note tells the reader to multiply importance by consistency, sum the products, and divide by 12, and it says a rough broad-public average composite is 61. That is a 12-domain instruction, not the 10-domain Wilson composite, and 61 is presented as a rough average, not a cutoff. — [Valued Living Questionnaire handout, stevenchayes.com](https://stevenchayes.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/The-Valued-Living-Questionnaire.pdf)

### Inferences
- A Bene in Mente composite around 60–64 matches the undergraduate samples in the Wilson manuscript. There is no published line above which the score is “clinical” or below which it is “healthy.” Importance sums in those samples were high (about 85–87 out of a 10–100 sum, roughly 8–9 per domain), so a low composite is more often low consistency than low importance.
- Do not compare a 10-domain mean-of-products score with a 12-domain score that was divided by 12. They are different formulas.

### Gaps
- Study 2 sample size was not extracted.
- The typeset 2010 Psychological Record PDF was not opened, so small differences from the manuscript cannot be ruled out.
- No clinical-population composite mean and no minimal important change were found.
- No Italian student mean was found.

## 9. Public Speaking Anxiety Scale (PSAS)

### Takeaway
Seventeen items, total 17–85, higher = more public-speaking anxiety. In the original US undergraduate sample the mean was 51.55 (SD 14.79) and the median was 52.5. The paper does not publish a clinical cutoff. A score of 55 is just above that sample’s median (between the 50th and 60th percentiles). Young Spotlight’s inclusion rule of 55 or higher is a study threshold, not a published case cutoff. It selects people who are average-to-high relative to US undergraduates, not people at the extreme.

### Cited Findings
- Bartholomay and Houlihan, Personality and Individual Differences, 2016, 94, 211–215. DOI 10.1016/j.paid.2016.01.026. Seventeen items, 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely). Five items are reverse-coded. Total, after reversal, ranges from 17 to 85. Higher = more public-speaking anxiety. The development sample was 375 undergraduate psychology students at a public Midwestern US university. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2016.01.026); figures read from an open copy of that article
- Overall scale mean in that sample: 51.55 (SD 14.79). Women M=53.30 (SD 14.97); men M=43.88 (SD 11.20); the gender difference was significant, F(1, 364)=23.87. — same article
- Percentiles printed with the scale (range stated as 17–85): 10th=31, 20th=38, 30th=43, 40th=48, 50th=52.5, 60th=57, 70th=60, 80th=64, 90th=71, 94th=73, 100th=84. The 94th percentile is labeled as a percentile. The article does not call 73, or 55, a diagnostic cutoff. — same article
- A later Spanish adaptation note says “a suggested cutoff score by the original version at 73 was found in the 96th percentile.” That sentence treats 73 as if the 2016 paper had proposed a cutoff. The 2016 percentile table lists 73 as the 94th percentile, not as a cutoff. — [Spanish PSAS note](https://www.academia.edu/37121331/Reliability_and_Factorial_Analysis_of_the_Public_Speaking_Anxiety_Scale_in_Spanish_PSAS_S); contradicted by the percentile table in Bartholomay and Houlihan 2016
- No row in the 2016 paper sets 55 as a cutoff. On the printed percentiles, 55 falls between the 50th (52.5) and the 60th (57).

### Inferences
- Young Spotlight’s inclusion threshold of 55 or higher is study-specific. On the only published distribution found here, 55 is a bit above the undergraduate average, not a severe-anxiety cutoff. The top of that student distribution is the 90th percentile at 71 and the 94th at 73.
- The norm sample is US undergraduates, not Norwegian adolescents aged 13–16. A score of 55 in a 13–16-year-old cannot be read as “average for that age” from these data. It can only be said that 55 was near the middle of a US student sample.

### Gaps
- No adolescent, Norwegian, or clinical-population PSAS mean was found.
- No minimal important change was found.
- The 2016 article was read from an open copy (pdfcoffee transcription of the journal pages). The DOI is the citation; the repository record is [Minnesota State, Mankato](https://cornerstone.lib.mnsu.edu/psyc_fac_pubs/283/).

## Cross-scale cautions for the catalogue reader

### Takeaway
Several catalogue rules are real but narrower than they sound: I-Sleep’s 8-point or below-11 ISI rule is a moderate-improvement / trial-entry rule, not remission to the no-insomnia band; SSS-8 “high or very high” is about the top 5–10% of German adults; PSAS 55 is near a student median; IPS totals and item means must not be compared; AAQ-II and VLQ do not have consensus clinical cutoffs.

### Cited Findings
- ISI bands and the 8-point and 11-point rules: see section 2, [Morin et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079939/) and [Gagnon et al. 2013](https://www.jabfm.org/content/26/6/701).
- SSS-8 high/very high = 12–32: [Gierk et al. 2014](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/1783305). Outpatient mean 12.96: [Gierk et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychores.2014.11.006).
- PSAS percentiles: Bartholomay and Houlihan 2016, [DOI 10.1016/j.paid.2016.01.026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2016.01.026).
- IPS item mean versus total: [Svartdal et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5005418/) versus [Caring Universities 2025](https://moodlift.nl/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Caring-Universities-sixth-survey-report-2025.pdf) and the [Steel handout](https://procrastinus.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/irrational-procrastination-scale.pdf).
- AAQ-II: [Bond et al. 2011](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/5531/2/AAQ-II_psychometrics_paper_%28accepted_version%29.pdf); [Wolgast 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25311291/); [Tyndall et al.](https://eprints.chi.ac.uk/id/eprint/3831/1/Accepted%20AAQ-II%20paper%20JCBS.pdf).
- VLQ: no cutoff in the [working manual](https://contextualscience.org/files/VLQ%20working%20manual%20v1-15-08.pdf).

### Inferences
- “Clinical” in these catalogues is often an author-chosen threshold (ISI <11, PSAS ≥55, IPS >31, SSS-8 high band, Danish SCAS method-C cut). Only the ISI bands, the SSS-8 percentile bands, and the SCAS T-score rules are widely published interpretive systems, and even those are not diagnostic criteria.

### Gaps
- Intervention score distributions themselves were not looked up. These notes are instrument benchmarks only.
