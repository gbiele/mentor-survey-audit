# Specialized scale benchmark scores

Notes for a report writer. Scope is the narrower and process-related instruments named in the catalogue (navHL, MHLS, SAES, GHSQ-V, social distance, HSE Indicator Tool, emotion regulation / ERQ, symptom NRS, CSQ-8, SUS, UEQ-S, REST@WORK, NCQ). A “normal” score below means a published comparison-group mean or an official occupational percentile band. It does not mean a clinical case threshold unless a source states one. No number below was estimated to fill a gap. Date of search: 29 October 2026.

## 1. Navigational health literacy (navHL), Nebolus

### Takeaway
Nebolus did not use the adult HLS19-NAV as its outcome. The trial instrument is a self-developed item pool (15 items, four-point agreement) with no published population mean and no clinical cutoff. The nearest published population scale is HLS19-NAV (Griese, Berens, Schaeffer and the HLS19 consortium): 12 items, scored to 0–100, German adult mean 41.6, and that figure must not be treated as the Nebolus pupil or student norm.

### Cited Findings
- The German Clinical Trials Register entry for the Nebolus school RCT (DRKS00033877) defines navigation health literacy as the ability and motivation to find, understand, evaluate, and apply health-related information and services provided in or by organisations or professionals in a person’s vicinity. It states that a quantitative instrument with 15 items was developed, answered on a four-point agreement scale, and given at baseline and one week after the intervention. — [DRKS00033877](https://drks.de/search/en/trial/DRKS00033877/details)
- The 2023 evaluation abstract (pupils, five secondary schools, Fulda rally, May 2022) says NavGK was captured with a self-developed item pool. Matched pre/post data were available for 148 pupils. Factor analysis produced three dimensions — Finden, Bewerten, Kommunizieren (finding, evaluating, communicating) — with satisfactory reliabilities. Group-by-time effects were reported for all three dimensions and the total, with partial eta-squared from 0.04 to 0.22. The abstract does not publish means, standard deviations, or a cutoff. — [Dadaczynski et al., Das Gesundheitswesen, 2023, DOI 10.1055/s-0043-1770574](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0043-1770574)
- The University of Potsdam Nebolus page says a second, individually randomised trial with 79 university students used the same kind of standardised pre/post instruments for a 4-hour intervention. That page does not publish scores. — [University of Potsdam, Nebolus](https://www.uni-potsdam.de/en/health-education/research/nebolus)
- The development paper describes Nebolus for adolescents aged 12 to 16 and, separately, cites adult German survey results: “more than 80% have difficulties in navigating the health care information environment,” attributed to the HL-NAV instrument and a representative German population survey. That sentence is about adults, not the 15-item pupil scale, and the paper does not give the pupil scale’s items or norms. — [Dadaczynski, Krah, Frank, Zügel-Hintz and Pöhlmann, Frontiers in Public Health, 2021, DOI 10.3389/fpubh.2021.752183](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2021.752183/full)
- HLS19-NAV, the population instrument led by the German HLS-GER 2 group (Griese, Berens, Schaeffer and colleagues), has 12 items. Each asks how easy a navigational task is: 4 very easy, 3 easy, 2 difficult, 1 very difficult. Items cover access, understand, appraise, and apply (3 items each). The published English items are: understand how the health care system works; judge which type of service is needed; judge insurance coverage; understand information on reforms; find out patient rights; decide for a particular service; find information on quality; judge whether a service will meet expectations; understand how to get an appointment; find support options for orientation; locate the right contact person; stand up for yourself if care does not meet your needs. — [Griese et al., validation paper, PMC9654211](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9654211/)
- The same validation paper states there is no separate clinical threshold. Scores discussed for population reporting were standardised to 0–100, with a higher score meaning higher navigational health literacy. Development history (scoping review, expert review, focus groups, 33 cognitive interviews, reduction from 15 draft items to 12) is in Griese et al. 2020. — [Griese, Berens, Nowak, Pelikan and Schaeffer, IJERPH, 2020, DOI 10.3390/ijerph17165731](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17165731); [PMC9654211](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9654211/)
- In the HLS19 international report the 0–100 score is the percentage of valid items answered “easy” or “very easy” (very easy/easy combined versus very difficult/difficult). Across the eight countries the mean of country means was 55.3. Country means ranged from 41.6 in Germany to 67.4 in Slovenia. The average standard deviation was 31.8, from 28.2 in Germany to 34.1 in France. Germany collected the scale by pen-and-paper interview, 13 December 2019 to 27 January 2020, total sample 2,143. The report does not apply a clinical cutoff. Distributions were not normal and showed a ceiling effect in most countries. — [HLS19 international report, chapter 10](https://ciencia.ucp.pt/ws/portalfiles/portal/63341554/HLS19_International_Report_242_273.pdf); German fieldwork details also in [PMC9654211](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9654211/)

### Inferences
- The catalogue wording (finding, evaluating, communicating health information, plus communicating with professionals) does not match a single published scoring key. The trial registry defines four operations (find, understand, evaluate, apply). The 2023 pupil analysis retained three factors (finding, evaluating, communicating). “Communicating with professionals” is therefore not a confirmed separate scored subscale in the published abstract.
- A German adult HLS19-NAV score of 41.6 means that, on average, about 42 percent of the 12 adult navigation items were rated easy or very easy. That is a general-population description for adults. It is not a pupil norm, not a university-student norm, and not a threshold for “adequate” navigational literacy on the Nebolus form.
- The “more than 80 percent have difficulties” sentence in the 2021 development paper is a person-level claim about an adult German survey. It is not the same statistic as the item-percentage mean of 41.6, and the two should not be arithmetically converted into each other.

### Gaps
- No published normal or clinical benchmark found for the Nebolus 15-item scale. Searches: “navigational health literacy Griese Berens Schaeffer scale”; “Nebolus navigational health literacy pupils Germany”. The evaluation abstract reports effects, not means. Item text of the 15-item pool was not published in the sources retrieved.
- No polytomous (1–4) population mean for Germany was extracted. The 41.6 figure is the dichotomised 0–100 index only.
- Whether the university trial (n = 79) used the identical 15 items was not confirmed beyond the Potsdam page’s statement that standardised instruments were used.

## 2. Mental Health Literacy Scale (MHLS; O’Connor and Casey, 2015)

### Takeaway
The original MHLS is a 35-item total from 35 to 160. Higher means greater mental health literacy. The 2015 “community” mean is 127.38, but that sample was first-year psychology students, not a general community and not patients. There is no universal clinical cutoff. Full-scale norms are only an approximate reference for Deep Breath, which partially adapted the questionnaires.

### Cited Findings
- O’Connor and Casey developed a 35-item univariate scale covering recognition of disorders (8 items), knowing where to seek information (4), risk factors and causes (2), self-treatment (2), professional help available (3), and attitudes that promote recognition or appropriate help-seeking (16). — [O’Connor and Casey, Psychiatry Research, 2015, DOI 10.1016/j.psychres.2015.05.064](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2015.05.064)
- Scoring used in later administration guides: items 1–15 are scored 1–4, with items 10, 12 and 15 reverse scored; items 16–35 are scored 1–5, with items 20–28 reverse scored; the total is the sum. Minimum 35, maximum 160. — [Salford PsyTech, MHLS](https://hub.salford.ac.uk/psytech/equipment-resources/questionnaire-battery/mental-health-literacy-scale-mhls/)
- In the derivation sample the authors called a community sample (n = 372; mean age 21.10, SD 6.27; 76.1 percent highest qualification a secondary-school certificate), the mean was 127.38 (SD 12.63; minimum 92; maximum 155; 95 percent CI 126.09 to 128.67). They later state that this sample was first-year university students in psychology courses and may have inflated mental health literacy relative to a broader community. — [O’Connor and Casey, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2015.05.064)
- Mental health professionals (n = 43; mean age 33.09) scored 145.49 (SD 7.19), higher than the student sample, d = 1.76. Within the student sample, people who had experienced a mental illness scored 130.97 (SD 13.21) versus 125.19 (SD 11.76) for those who had not; people who had seen a mental health practitioner scored 133.53 (SD 12.02) versus 123.88 (SD 11.61); people with a family member or friend with a mental illness scored 129.53 (SD 12.12) versus 122.69 (SD 12.49). The scale was not correlated with psychological distress. The paper presents the MHLS for comparing people or groups and for evaluating programmes. It does not define a clinical cutoff. — [O’Connor and Casey, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2015.05.064)

### Inferences
- A score near 127 is what first-year Australian psychology students averaged in 2015, not what “healthy” or “clinical” means. Professionals averaged about 145 on the same total. Neither figure is a case threshold.
- Because Deep Breath’s questionnaires were only partly adapted from the MHLS, a programme score cannot be placed on the 35–160 scale unless the adaptation kept all 35 items and the original scoring. The 127.38 mean is a rough external reference only.

### Gaps
- No patient or clinical-caseness norm, and no universal cutoff, in the 2015 paper. No Hungarian or social-media-campaign norm was retrieved. Search: “O’Connor Casey 2015 Mental Health Literacy Scale means”.

## 3. SAES (SInAPSi Academic Engagement Scale)

### Takeaway
SAES is a published local instrument from the SInAPSi centre at the University of Naples Federico II, not an unpublished in-house quiz, but it has no clinical cutoff and no norm outside its validation samples. Published comparison values are six factor means in Italian science students, mostly in the range of about 3 to 4 on whatever item metric the paper used.

### Cited Findings
- Freda, De Luca Picione, Esposito, Ragozini and Testa developed SAES inside a project coordinated by SInAPSi (Services for active participation and inclusion of university students) at the authors’ university. The final scale is 29 items in six factors: (1) university value and sense of belonging; (2) perception of the capability to persist in the university choice; (3) value of the university course; (4) engagement with university professors; (5) engagement with university peers; (6) relationships between university and the relational net. Some items are reverse scored. The paper does not state a clinical cutoff. — [Freda et al., Current Psychology, 2021, DOI 10.1007/s12144-021-02189-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12144-021-02189-2)
- Group 1 was 680 first-year science students at that university (88.1 percent aged 18–20; 53.4 percent female; biology, biotechnologies, chemistry, physics, informatics, mathematics). Group 2 was 312 third-year biology and biotechnology students (73.7 percent female) used for confirmatory factor analysis. — [Freda et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12144-021-02189-2)
- Table 2 factor means (SD), Group 1 then Group 2: belonging 4.08 (0.59) and 4.11 (0.64); persistence 4.20 (0.71) and 4.09 (0.83); value of course 3.83 (0.75) and 3.91 (0.72); professors 3.22 (0.66) and 2.89 (0.76); peers 3.73 (0.79) and 3.69 (0.83); relational net 3.12 (0.97) and 3.27 (0.98). Cronbach alphas for Group 1 were 0.80, 0.74, 0.90, 0.70, 0.82 and 0.76. — [Freda et al., 2021, open PDF](https://www.iris.unina.it/retrieve/80b8036d-a1c1-4951-aedb-09c4adfd4b05/Freda_et_al-2021-Current_Psychology.pdf)
- In Group 1, men scored higher than women on value of the course (3.95 vs 3.72) and engagement with professors (3.32 vs 3.14). Women scored higher on the relational-net factor (3.30 vs 2.92). Good performers (more than 40 of 60 ECTS) scored higher than other students on belonging (good performers M = 4.23, SD 0.53) and on value of the course. Low performers (fewer than 20 of 60 ECTS) scored lower on persistence (M = 4.01, SD 0.80) and on the relational-net factor. — [Freda et al., 2021](https://www.iris.unina.it/retrieve/80b8036d-a1c1-4951-aedb-09c4adfd4b05/Freda_et_al-2021-Current_Psychology.pdf)
- A later SInAPSi counselling write-up administered SAES before and after four-session psychodynamic counselling and reported improvement on most SAES dimensions. That record does not publish SAES means. — [University of Naples IRIS, 11588/1011618](https://iris.unina.it/handle/11588/1011618)

### Inferences
- For Bene in Mente, the relevant published comparison is these Naples science-student factor means, especially Group 1 if participants are early in the degree. A “high” engagement score in this literature is a higher factor mean. The paper never converts that into a normal-versus-clinical classification.
- The scale is multidimensional. A single total was not the statistic reported in Table 2, so a programme total, if one is computed, has no published benchmark in this paper.

### Gaps
- The response anchors (for example 1–5 agreement) were not stated in the text retrieved. Means are reported, but the exact point scale was not. Search: “SInAPSi academic engagement scale SAES Naples”.
- No national Italian norm, no non-science faculty norm, and no clinical cutoff were found.
- Final item counts per factor after the reduction to 29 items were not fully extracted (the appendix lists items; the exploratory counts are not the final counts).

## 4. GHSQ-V (vignette General Help-Seeking Questionnaire)

### Takeaway
There is no normal or clinical cutoff. The questionnaire is a set of 1–7 intentions, one per help source (and per problem), not a single health score. In the original Australian high-school sample, personal-emotional problems were directed toward friends and parents; a GP was unlikely. The vignette form uses the same idea with symptom scenarios. Means are per source.

### Cited Findings
- Wilson, Deane, Ciarrochi and Rickwood describe the GHSQ as a matrix. The probe is: “If you were having [problem-type], how likely is it that you would seek help from the following people?” Each source is rated from 1 (extremely unlikely) to 7 (extremely likely). Higher means a stronger intention for that source. Sources and problems are meant to be changed for the sample. The original study used personal-emotional problems and suicidal thoughts. Help sources included partner, friend, parent, other family, mental health professional, phone helpline, doctor/GP, teacher, pastor/priest, youth worker, and “would not seek help”. The authors say intentions can be looked at by source, or by combining sources across problems. They do not define a total “normal” score or a clinical cutoff. — [Wilson et al., Canadian Journal of Counselling and Psychotherapy, high-school psychometric paper; PDF](https://josephciarrochi.com/downloads/2005%20-%20Measuring%20help-seeking%20intentions%20Properties%20of%20the%20general%20help-seeking%20questionnaire.pdf); [journal record](https://cjc-rcc.ucalgary.ca/article/view/58748); [original-version form, University of Wollongong](https://documents.uow.edu.au/content/groups/public/@web/@gsm/documents/doc/uow090184.pdf)
- In that high-school sample (N = 218), mean intentions (SE) for personal-emotional problems were: friend 5.13 (0.13), parent 4.84 (0.15), partner 4.77 (0.16), other family 3.75 (0.16), would not seek help 2.86 (0.18), doctor/GP 2.73 (0.14), mental health professional 2.68 (0.13), teacher 2.64 (0.14), phone helpline 2.14 (0.12), youth worker 2.08 (0.13), pastor/priest 1.77 (0.12). Informal sources clustered toward 5–7 and formal sources toward 1–3. Friend was higher than every other source. — [Wilson et al. PDF, Table 1](https://josephciarrochi.com/downloads/2005%20-%20Measuring%20help-seeking%20intentions%20Properties%20of%20the%20general%20help-seeking%20questionnaire.pdf)
- For suicidal thoughts in the same sample, means were lower for friend (4.34), parent (3.56) and partner (4.03), and “would not seek help” was 2.61. Doctor/GP stayed 2.63. — [Wilson et al. PDF, Table 1](https://josephciarrochi.com/downloads/2005%20-%20Measuring%20help-seeking%20intentions%20Properties%20of%20the%20general%20help-seeking%20questionnaire.pdf)
- The vignette version (GHSQ-V) asks the same likelihood question after a short scenario of a young person. A Chilean adolescent adaptation (14–19 years, n = 793, Talca) describes seven problem types: stress, anxiety, depression, suicidal ideation, substance abuse, psychosis, and physical illness. An example vignette begins “In the last two weeks, Juan has found it difficult to relax…”. Sources named in that paper include partner, friend, parent, other family, and mental health professional. Exploratory factor analysis separated informal and formal sources. The paper treats each problem area as its own scale, following Wilson. — [Chilean GHSQ-V validation, English PDF, Revista Chilena de Pediatría](http://www.scielo.cl/pdf/rcp/v88n3/en_art03.pdf); Spanish record [DOI 10.4067/s0370-41062017000300003](https://doi.org/10.4067/s0370-41062017000300003)
- That English PDF says the Chilean Likert ran from 1 = extremely probable to 7 = extremely unlikely. The original GHSQ and the Wollongong form run from 1 = extremely unlikely to 7 = extremely likely. — [Chilean PDF](http://www.scielo.cl/pdf/rcp/v88n3/en_art03.pdf); [Wollongong form](https://documents.uow.edu.au/content/groups/public/@web/@gsm/documents/doc/uow090184.pdf)

### Inferences
- For Mindhelper (Danish youth 13–20), a published comparison is the Australian high-school pattern: friends and parents above the midpoint (4) for an emotional problem; GP, mental health professional, teacher, and helpline below 3; “nobody” also below 3, meaning the typical student did not say they were likely to seek help from no one. These are intentions, not behaviour, and they are not Danish norms.
- “Would not seek help” is scored in the same 1–7 direction as the other rows (higher = more likely to use that option). It is not a reversed item that can be added into a “help-seeking total” without a separate decision to reverse it. The original paper does not recommend one total.
- The reversed anchors in the English Chilean paper are likely a translation inconsistency. Until the Spanish wording is checked, the original 1 = extremely unlikely direction is the one that matches Wilson’s instrument. Do not average Chilean and Australian numbers if the poles differ.

### Gaps
- No Danish youth means and no Mindhelper source list were retrieved. No clinical cutoff exists in the sources read.
- The bibliographic year of the original paper is unstable across citations (the PDF is the 2005 manuscript; the Chilean reference list dates the journal article to 2007, volume 39). The journal URL above is the stable record.
- Item-level means for the seven GHSQ-V vignettes were not extracted.

## 5. Social distance (Nebolus)

### Takeaway
Nebolus’s exact social-distance form is not specified, so no numeric benchmark can be matched with certainty. The form that can be tied to adolescent mental-health studies is a five-item youth adaptation of Link’s scale: 1 to 4, higher meaning greater unwillingness to have contact. That adaptation has no clinical cutoff.

### Cited Findings
- Social distance in mental-illness research comes from Bogardus’s willingness-to-interact idea, later used with psychiatric vignettes. Link and colleagues’ stigma review describes social distance as willingness to accept relationships of increasing closeness with a person described in a vignette. Scales in this family usually show internal consistency from about 0.75 to above 0.90. The same review states two limits: social-desirability under-reporting, and the gap between a stated intention and actual behaviour. It does not offer a normal or clinical cutoff. — [Link, Yang, Phelan and Collins, Measuring Mental Illness Stigma, PDF hosted at Montefiore Einstein](https://montefioreeinstein.org/documents/Measuring-Mental-Illness-Stigma.pdf)
- The adult form used in Australian national surveys is the five-item scale from Link et al. (1999). After a vignette, respondents rate willingness to (1) move next door, (2) spend an evening socialising, (3) make friends, (4) work closely on a job, and (5) have the person marry into the family. Each item is 1 = definitely willing to 4 = definitely unwilling. Greater unwillingness means greater desired distance. — [Yap, Mackinnon, Reavley and Jorm, International Journal of Methods in Psychiatric Research, 2014, DOI 10.1002/mpr.1433](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6878256/)
- The youth form in the same paper was adapted for young people (Jorm and Wright, 2008) from Link et al. (1999). It was used with respondents aged 15–25 who heard an age-matched vignette (15-year-old protagonist for ages 15–17; 21-year-old for ages 18–25). The five items are willingness to (1) go out with the person on the weekend, (2) invite them to your house, (3) go to their house, (4) work closely with them on a project, and (5) develop a close friendship. The scale is 1 = yes, definitely to 4 = definitely not. All five items loaded on one factor. Vignettes included depression, depression with suicidal thoughts, depression with alcohol misuse, social phobia, psychosis/schizophrenia, and PTSD. The paper’s figure compares disorders, but the numeric means sit in the figure and were not printed in the text. Qualitatively, early schizophrenia and depression with alcohol misuse elicited the most stigma on the youth dangerous/unpredictable factor; the social-distance panel is in the same figure. — [Yap et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6878256/)

### Inferences
- If Nebolus used a sum of five youth items, the possible range would be 5–20 with higher meaning more distance. That range is a mechanical consequence of the 1–4 items above. It is not evidence that Nebolus used this form. Because the intervention text does not name Link, Bogardus, Jorm, or the item set, a catalogue score cannot be read against these items.
- There is still no “normal” distance. Studies compare vignettes and demographic groups. They do not classify a respondent as stigmatising above a cut point.

### Gaps
- Nebolus’s exact variant is unspecified. No Nebolus social-distance mean was found. Searches: “Link social distance scale mental illness adolescents items”; the Nebolus papers retrieved for section 1 do not specify the stigma items.
- Numeric youth means by diagnosis were not extracted (figure only). No German adolescent mean for this five-item form was retrieved in this pass.

## 6. HSE Management Standards Indicator Tool

### Takeaway
This is an occupational stress-risk tool, not a clinical symptom scale. Analysed scores run from 1 to 5, and a lower score means worse working conditions. The official benchmark compares the organisation’s mean on each standard with percentile cut-points (below the 20th, up to the 50th, up to the 80th, at or above the 80th). Those cut-points differ by standard. There is no clinical cutoff.

### Cited Findings
- The HSE Management Standards Analysis Tool scores seven areas: Demands, Control, Managerial support, Peer support, Relationships, Role, and Change. Colour is assigned by comparing the organisation’s result with a benchmark. The manual states that scores range from 1 to 5 and that a lower score indicates poor performance or a potential problem area. For the bullying item (question 21), a raw analysed score of 1, 2 or 3 is described as always, often, or sometimes bullied, which shows that in the analysed metric 1 is the adverse end. — [HSE Management Standards Analysis Tool User Manual, copy retrieved as analysis_tool_manual.pdf](https://www.alanbradshaw.uk/uploads/2/7/0/6/2706840/analysis_tool_manual.pdf)
- The manual’s colour key is: below the 20th percentile, urgent action (red); at or above the 20th and below the 50th, improvement needed (amber/yellow); at or above the 50th and below the 80th, good performance but potential improvement (aqua); at or above the 80th, doing very well, maintain performance (green). The manual says percentile boundaries differ by standard, so a lower average can still be a relatively better percentile. Suggested longer-term targets are the 80th percentile of the benchmark table. An organisation already at or above the 80th is told to set its own targets. — [Analysis Tool User Manual](https://www.alanbradshaw.uk/uploads/2/7/0/6/2706840/analysis_tool_manual.pdf)
- Two benchmarks are built into that tool. HSE recommended the organisational set in most cases: surveys from 136 organisations that used the Indicator Tool. The older set is the 2004 Psychosocial Working Conditions survey, a household survey of about 1,800 workers in Britain, which the manual says included the 35 Indicator Tool items. The manual points to the 2004 report at www.hse.gov.uk/statistics/causdis/pwc2004.pdf. The 35 items originally represented six Management Standards areas; the analysis tool reports seven scores because managerial support and peer support are separate. — [Analysis Tool User Manual](https://www.alanbradshaw.uk/uploads/2/7/0/6/2706840/analysis_tool_manual.pdf)
- Published colour boundaries from the 136-organisation set (these are the 20th, 50th and 80th percentile cuts in Table 2 of the manual). Red if below the first number; yellow from that number up to but not including the second; aqua from the second up to but not including the third; green at or above the third. Demands: 2.9387 / 3.1024 / 3.2937. Control: 3.2240 / 3.4741 / 3.7208. Managers’ support: 3.2720 / 3.4603 / 3.6500. Peer support: 3.6270 / 3.7800 / 3.8892. Relationships: 3.6115 / 3.8499 / 4.0381. Role: 4.0356 / 4.1803 / 4.3117. Change: 2.7910 / 3.0428 / 3.2400. — [Analysis Tool User Manual, Table 2](https://www.alanbradshaw.uk/uploads/2/7/0/6/2706840/analysis_tool_manual.pdf)
- A later HSE Books Stress Indicator Tool benchmarking report (all-industry data: 83,452 responses, 329 assessments) publishes a different distribution. All-industry means: Demands 3.25, Control 3.67, Managers’ support 3.78, Peer support 3.99, Relationships 4.12, Role 4.16, Change 3.25. All-industry 20th / 50th / 80th percentiles: Demands 3.06 / 3.25 / 3.50; Control 3.44 / 3.74 / 3.91; Managers’ support 3.61 / 3.81 / 3.97; Peer support 3.83 / 4.00 / 4.12; Relationships 3.94 / 4.16 / 4.31; Role 3.98 / 4.16 / 4.36; Change 2.98 / 3.29 / 3.55. The report says a score at the 5th percentile means only 5 percent of comparable organisations scored lower. It also warns that year-to-year changes in the mean mostly reflect which organisations took part, not a national trend. — [The 2026 HSE Benchmarking Report, HSE Books](https://books.hse.tso.co.uk/sites/default/files/2026-09/The%202026%20HSE%20Benchmarking%20Report.pdf)
- Where the figures live: the percentile tables used by the free Analysis Tool are inside that tool and are printed in its user manual (136 organisations, and the 2004 survey). Current commercial all-industry percentiles are in the HSE Books Stress Indicator Tool benchmarking reports (the 2026 report above; HSE Books also lists supporting resources at books.hse.gov.uk). — [Analysis Tool User Manual](https://www.alanbradshaw.uk/uploads/2/7/0/6/2706840/analysis_tool_manual.pdf); [HSE Books supporting resources](https://books.hse.gov.uk/stress-indicator-tool-supporting-resources); [2026 report](https://books.hse.tso.co.uk/sites/default/files/2026-09/The%202026%20HSE%20Benchmarking%20Report.pdf)

### Inferences
- Mindliven’s labels map onto these seven scores: Job Demands = Demands plus Relationships; Job Resources = Control, Role, Change, Supervisor Support (the HSE name is Managerial / Managers’ support), and Peer Support. The benchmark is still seven separate means, not two combined totals. A combined “demands” or “resources” total has no official percentile in the manual.
- On this metric, a higher mean is the favourable direction (better reported working conditions), which is the opposite of a symptom scale. Role and peer support sit higher in absolute numbers than Demands and Change; the manual explicitly says not to treat a lower average as worse without the percentile.
- The 136-organisation cuts and the 2026 all-industry cuts are different reference populations. For example, the 2026 20th percentile for Demands is 3.06, which would already sit near the old 50th percentile (3.10). Quoting one set against the other will mis-classify an organisation.

### Gaps
- The user manual’s benchmark is the mean score against percentiles. A single “percent of responses in the desired category”, compared with the 20th/50th/80th, was not the method in the manual sections read. The manual does report a percentage for one item: how many staff scored in the bullied range on question 21. If Mindliven reports a percent-favourable figure, that is a local summary, and no official percent-favourable target was found in this pass.
- Respondent-facing anchor words (the usual Never / Seldom / Sometimes / Often / Always) were not re-read from the questionnaire PDF on hse.gov.uk in this pass. The analysed direction (1 = adverse, 5 = favourable) is taken from the manual’s bullying example and from the statement that lower scores are worse.
- The manual copy used here is a third-party upload of the HSE user manual, not a page on hse.gov.uk. The percentile numbers inside it are those of the HSE tool as printed in that manual.

## 7. Emotion regulation (reappraisal and suppression; ERQ if that is the scale)

### Takeaway
Mindliven’s phrase “emotion regulation strategies, assessed through reappraisal and suppression” matches the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, but the intervention text does not name the ERQ. If it is the ERQ, each subscale is an average (or sum) of 1–7 items, higher reappraisal is generally the more favourable direction, higher suppression is generally not, and there is no clinical cutoff. US student item-means are about 4.6 for reappraisal and about 3.1–3.6 for suppression.

### Cited Findings
- The ERQ (Gross and John, 2003) is 10 items, 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree, no reversals. Reappraisal items are 1, 3, 5, 7, 8 and 10. Suppression items are 2, 4, 6 and 9. Item order is fixed because items 1 and 3 introduce “positive emotion” and “negative emotion”. — [Gross and John ERQ form, Stanford](https://spl.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19321/files/media/file/english_0.pdf); [Gross and John, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2003, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.85.2.348](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.2.348)
- In the US undergraduate samples, overall reappraisal means were 4.60 (SD 0.94) for men and 4.61 (SD 1.02) for women, with no consistent gender difference. Overall suppression means were 3.64 (SD 1.11) for men and 3.14 (SD 1.18) for women. Men were higher on suppression in every sample, by about half a standard deviation (mean d about 0.47). The two scales were essentially uncorrelated (mean r about 0.01). Reappraisal was positively related to well-being; suppression was negatively related. Three-month test-retest reliability was 0.69 for both scales. — [Gross and John, 2003, PDF](https://onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca/implicitassociationtestsyessir/wp-content/uploads/sites/9812/2025/12/gross-john-2003.pdf)
- A German community study (N = 2,524; mean age 49.4, SD 18.2; 55.5 percent female; ADM sample compared with Federal Statistical Office figures) used the German ERQ. Internal consistency was 0.82 for reappraisal and 0.76 for suppression. Reappraisal correlated −0.10 with HADS anxiety and −0.16 with HADS depression. Suppression correlated +0.08 with anxiety and +0.16 with depression. Norms are percentile tables by age and gender. The authors say it remains unclear whether reappraisal is protective and suppression unfavourable, or whether distress leads people to suppress. They do not publish a clinical cutoff. Suppression sum scores (not 1–7 item means) were higher in men (M = 18.28, SD 4.90) than women (M = 17.76, SD 5.11). — [Wiltink et al., GMS Psycho-Social-Medicine, 2011, DOI 10.3205/psm000078](https://www.egms.de/static/de/journals/psm/2011-8/psm000078.shtml)
- The German student translation (three student samples, total n = 454) replicated the two factors. The abstract and the record retrieved do not report subscale means. Alphas were about 0.74–0.76. — [Abler and Kessler, Diagnostica, 2009, DOI 10.1026/0012-1924.55.3.144](https://doi.org/10.1026/0012-1924.55.3.144)

### Inferences
- Possible ranges, if scored as item means, are 1–7 for each subscale. If scored as sums, reappraisal is 6–42 and suppression is 4–28. Gross and John’s published means are item means. Wiltink’s 18-point suppression figures are sums (a 1–7 item mean cannot be 18). The two must not be compared until both are on the same metric. The German 1–7 means are in Wiltink’s Table 3 and were not extracted here.
- “Generally favourable” for reappraisal and “generally not” for suppression is the direction of the well-being and depression correlations. It is not a cut score. A suppression score above 3.64 is above the US male student mean, not a clinical flag.

### Gaps
- Mindliven does not name the ERQ. Another reappraisal/suppression item set is possible. No Mindliven scoring note was found.
- German community item-means (1–7) and the percentile norm tables were not extracted. Abler and Kessler’s student means were not in the abstract. No clinical cutoff was found. Search: “Gross John 2003 ERQ reappraisal suppression means”; “ERQ German validation Abler Kessler reappraisal suppression mean”.

## 8. NRS 0–10 for symptom intensity and interference (eAsy / My Symptoms)

### Takeaway
This is a single numeric rating, not a multi-item questionnaire. Anchors are 0 = none and 10 = the worst imaginable. Bands such as 1–3 mild, 4–6 moderate, and 7–10 severe are common conventions in pain research, and other studies place the cuts elsewhere. They are not a diagnosis. The intervention’s own inclusion rule is an average of intensity and of interference of 3 or higher.

### Cited Findings
- A numeric rating scale asks for one number from 0 to 10. In pain research, 0 is typically “no pain” and 10 is “the most intense pain imaginable” or “pain as bad as it could be”. The same format is used for intensity and, separately, for interference. — [Shirley Ryan AbilityLab, Numeric Pain Rating Scale, citing Williamson and Hoggart](https://www.sralab.org/rehabilitation-measures/numeric-pain-rating-scale)
- In young people with physical disabilities (n = 113; ages 8–20; mean age 14.19), the cut scheme that best tracked pain interference for average pain was 0–3 mild, 4–6 moderate, and 7–10 severe. For worst pain the best scheme was 0–4 mild, 5–6 moderate, and 7–10 severe. Ratings were 7-day recall, 0 = no pain, 10 = pain as bad as could be. — [Miró, de la Vega, Solé and colleagues, Disability and Rehabilitation, 2016, DOI 10.1080/09638288.2016.1185469](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5553452/)
- In adults with chronic musculoskeletal pain, the scheme that best tracked interference was different: NRS scores of 5 or below corresponded to mild interference, 6–7 to moderate, and 8 or above to severe. Among patients with low catastrophising, the scheme shifted to 3 or below mild, 4–6 moderate, and 7 or above severe. The authors cite the wider literature: the mild/moderate cut usually falls between 3 and 4, and the moderate/severe cut between 6 and 8, and they state that simply splitting 0–10 into three equal parts is not a valid method. Serlin and colleagues’ 1995 cancer-pain method is the source of this interference-based approach. — [Boonstra et al., Frontiers in Psychology, 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01466/full)
- A multiple-sclerosis sample produced yet another pair of schemes: average pain 0–2 mild, 3–5 moderate, 6–10 severe; worst pain 0–4 mild, 5–7 moderate, 8–10 severe. — [Alschuler et al., PMC3473137](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3473137/)

### Inferences
- For eAsy / My Symptoms (persistent physical symptoms, ages 15–65), the catalogue rule “average intensity and interference of 3 or higher” is an inclusion rule for that intervention. On the common 0–3 / 4–6 / 7–10 convention, 3 is still inside mild. On the Boonstra musculoskeletal scheme, 3 is also inside mild. A score of 3 is therefore enough to enter that intervention and is still described as mild in the two schemes just cited. It is not a diagnosis of a moderate or severe disorder.
- Intensity and interference are two ratings. A person can be included because both averages are at least 3. One number should not be treated as covering both.

### Gaps
- No published NRS norm specific to the Danish My Symptoms programmes was searched for beyond the general pain-NRS literature. No diagnostic cutoff exists; the bands above are interference-based classifications from particular patient groups.
- Search: “numeric rating scale pain 0-10 mild moderate severe cutoff”.

## 9. CSQ-8 and SUS (brief)

### Takeaway
CSQ-8 runs from 8 to 32; higher means more satisfied with a service. SUS runs from 0 to 100; about 68 is an average usability score, and a converted score of 85.98 sits on the “Excellent” adjective mean (85.5), not on a clinical scale. The GetStarted figures in the brief (CSQ-8 M = 23.48, SD 3.23; SUS-10 mean 34.39, described as 85.98/100) were not re-extracted from a paper in this search.

### Cited Findings
- The assignment states that GetStarted (Dutch students) reported CSQ-8 M = 23.48 (SD 3.23) and a SUS-10 mean of 34.39 described as equivalent to 85.98/100. This search did not open the GetStarted paper.
- SUS yields a score from 0 (negative) to 100 (positive). Bangor, Kortum and Miller added an adjective rating to 959 SUS surveys. Mean SUS by adjective: Worst imaginable 12.5 (SD 13.1, n = 4); Awful 20.3 (SD 11.3, n = 22); Poor 35.7 (SD 12.6, n = 72); OK 50.9 (SD 13.8, n = 211); Good 71.4 (SD 11.6, n = 345); Excellent 85.5 (SD 10.4, n = 289); Best imaginable 90.9 (SD 13.4, n = 16). Adjectives differed except Worst imaginable versus Awful. The correlation between the adjective and the SUS score was r = 0.822. These are mean scores of people who chose each word, not hard cut-points. — [Bangor, Kortum and Miller, Journal of Usability Studies, 2009, 4(3), 114–123](http://uxpajournal.org/wp-content/uploads/pdf/JUS_Bangor_May2009.pdf)
- A large-database summary states that the average SUS (50th percentile) is 68, so a score above 68 is above average. The same summary says scores above 85 are associated with “Excellent”, and that “Good” was just above average at 71. — [Sauro, MeasuringU, “5 Ways to Interpret a SUS Score”](https://measuringu.com/interpret-sus-score/)
- The 0–100 SUS score is a linear rescaling of the ten recoded items. Each item contributes 0 to 4 after recoding, the ten contributions sum to 0–40, and that sum is multiplied by 2.5. 34.39 × 2.5 = 85.975, which rounds to 85.98. A figure of 34.39 is therefore the 0–40 sum, not a score already on the 0–100 scale. Read as if it were already out of 100, 34.39 would sit next to Bangor’s “Poor” mean (35.7), which is the wrong comparison. — arithmetic from the published 0–40 × 2.5 conversion that produces the 0–100 score described by [Bangor et al., 2009](http://uxpajournal.org/wp-content/uploads/pdf/JUS_Bangor_May2009.pdf)

### Inferences
- 85.98 is essentially the same number as the mean SUS of people who called a product “Excellent” (85.5). It is above the “Good” adjective mean (71.4) and above the 50th-percentile average of 68. “Above 80 is excellent” is a rough gloss. Bangor did not publish a cut at 80; the Excellent adjective’s own mean was 85.5.
- CSQ-8 at 23.48 on an 8–32 scale is toward the satisfied side of the midpoint (midpoint of the possible range is 20) but is not interpretable as a clinical outcome. Satisfaction and usability are not symptom scores. A high SUS or CSQ-8 does not mean low distress.

### Gaps
- The 1979 CSQ-8 development paper (Larsen, Attkisson, Hargreaves and Nguyen) was not opened in this pass. The 8–32 range and “higher = more satisfied” direction are the standard description named in the brief; they were not re-derived from Attkisson’s text here. No CSQ-8 clinical cutoff was sought, by design.
- The GetStarted means were not independently verified. Brooke’s 1996 book chapter, the primary SUS source, was not opened; the 0–100 range and adjective table were taken from Bangor et al. 2009, and the average of 68 from MeasuringU.

## 10. UEQ-S (Nebolus)

### Takeaway
UEQ-S is a usability and user-experience measure, not a health scale. Item and scale means run from −3 to +3. A published benchmark, adapted from the full UEQ, labels results from bad to excellent. There is no clinical cutoff.

### Cited Findings
- The short form has 8 semantic-differential items: 4 for pragmatic quality and 4 for hedonic quality. The overall score is the mean of the 8 items. The positive poles used in the short form are supportive, easy, efficient, clear, exciting, interesting, inventive, and leading edge. Values are interpreted on the usual UEQ metric of −3 to +3. — [Hinderks, Schrepp and Thomaschewski, 2024, DOI 10.9781/ijimai.2024.08.005](https://doi.org/10.9781/ijimai.2024.08.005)
- Because raw item data for the full benchmark were often unavailable, the short-form benchmark was calculated by transforming the full UEQ scale means (9,905 persons, 246 products). The resulting bands are: Excellent (among the best 10 percent): pragmatic quality greater than 1.73, hedonic quality greater than 1.55, overall greater than 1.58. Good: pragmatic 1.55 to 1.73, hedonic 1.25 to 1.55, overall 1.40 to 1.58. Above average: pragmatic 1.15 to 1.54, hedonic 0.88 to 1.24, overall 1.02 to 1.39. Below average: pragmatic 0.73 to 1.14, hedonic 0.57 to 0.87, overall 0.68 to 1.01. Bad (among the worst 25 percent): pragmatic less than 0.73, hedonic less than 0.57, overall less than 0.68. The authors describe this as a reasonable benchmark until a benchmark built only on UEQ-S raw data exists. — [Hinderks, Schrepp and Thomaschewski, 2018, DOI 10.5220/0007188303730377, PDF](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2018/71883/71883.pdf)

### Inferences
- A positive overall mean is a favourable user-experience result relative to other interactive products. It says nothing about navigational health literacy or stigma, which Nebolus also measured.

### Gaps
- No Nebolus UEQ-S mean was retrieved in this pass. The benchmark is not an independent short-form norm; it is a transformation of the longer UEQ benchmark.

## 11. REST@WORK and the Nature Contact Questionnaire (NCQ)

### Takeaway
No published normal or clinical benchmark was found for an instrument named REST@WORK. The Nature Contact Questionnaire has a workplace development mean, but it is an exposure checklist, not a symptom scale, and it has no clinical cutoff.

### Cited Findings
- Search for “REST@WORK restorativeness Nature Contact Questionnaire NCQ means” did not return a scored restorativeness scale under the name REST@WORK with sample means. One hit is an ETUI handbook whose extracted text describes a Rest@work questionnaire about employee participation in work-stress assessment; it is not a published norm table. — [ETUI REST@Work handbook, Greek PDF](https://www.etui.org/sites/default/files/ez_import/REST@Work_Handbook_Greek.pdf)
- A different, newer scale, the Restorativeness at Work Scale (R@WS), was published in 2026. It is a 13-item, four-factor measure (Being-Away, Fascination, Coherence, Scope) of the restorative quality of physical workplaces, based on Attention Restoration Theory. The abstract does not report means or a cutoff. The name is R@WS, not REST@WORK, so it should not be assumed to be Mindliven’s instrument. — [Journal of Environmental Psychology, 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.jenvp.2026.103005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2026.103005)
- The Nature Contact Questionnaire (Largo-Wight, Chen, Dodd and Weiler) was developed as a self-report of actual nature exposure at work. In the development study, 503 office staff (about a 30 percent response; mean age 42, SD 12; 92.9 percent women) completed it. The paper describes 16 items in outdoor, indoor, and indirect contact, with higher scores meaning more contact. A reliability table in the same paper reports a total based on 10 items: mean 22.5 (SD 8.3). Subtotals in that table: outdoor 5.6 (SD 3.4, 3 items), indoor 7.5 (SD 5.0, 3 items), indirect 5.9 (SD 3.2, 3 items). There is no clinical cutoff. The score is exposure, not symptoms. — [Largo-Wight et al., Work, 2011, DOI 10.3233/WOR-2011-1253](https://doi.org/10.3233/WOR-2011-1253)
- A companion health study of the same office sample describes the NCQ as 16 items with a possible total from 16 to 96, and subscales for outdoor, indoor, and indirect contact. The text extract confirms the range and the sample (n = 503) but does not print the 16-item total mean (the results table was not in the text extract). High versus low contact was defined as one standard deviation above or below the sample mean, which is a sample split, not a norm or a clinical cut. — [Largo-Wight et al., Public Health Reports, PMC3072911](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3072911/)

### Inferences
- If Mindliven’s NCQ is this instrument, the only numeric comparison retrieved is the office-staff development mean of 22.5 on the 10-item total in Table 7 of the Work paper. That number must not be placed on the 16–96 range of the companion paper. The two papers do not describe the same total.
- REST@WORK and R@WS should be kept apart until Mindliven’s item text is matched to one of them.

### Gaps
- No published normal or clinical benchmark found for REST@WORK. Searches used the instrument name together with “restorativeness” and with the NCQ. The 2026 R@WS abstract contains no means.
- The 16-item NCQ total mean was not in the text extracted from the health paper. No European or Mindliven-sample NCQ mean was found.
