# Wellbeing and health-related quality-of-life scale benchmarks

Notes compiled 29 October 2026 for judging catalogue outcome scores. Higher is better on every scale below. Numbers are only those retrieved from the cited sources. Where a table cell was not in the fetched text, it is listed as a gap rather than estimated.

## WHO-5 Well-Being Index

### Takeaway
On the 0–100 percentage score, general-population means in Denmark and Germany sit around 68–70. A percentage score of 50 or below (integer raw score 12 or below) is the usual screening cut for poor well-being and possible depression; scores around 28 or below resemble major depression. A 10-point change on the 0–100 scale is the working threshold for a clinically relevant change, but it is not a formally established minimal important difference.

### Cited Findings
- Full name: 5-item World Health Organization Well-Being Index (WHO-5). Five positively worded items, past 14 days. Each item is scored 0 (at no time) to 5 (all of the time). Raw total 0–25. Percentage score = raw × 4, range 0–100. Higher is better. Zero is worst possible well-being; 100 is best. — [WHO-5 scoring sheet](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/mental-health/who-5_english-original4da539d6ed4b49389e3afe47cda2326a.pdf); [Topp, Østergaard, Søndergaard and Bech, Psychother Psychosom 2015;84:167–176](https://doi.org/10.1159/000376585)
- The WHO sheet says a percentage score below 50, or a raw score below 13, is a suggested cut-off for poor mental well-being and an indication for further assessment. Topp et al. recommend a cut-off of ≤50 when screening for clinical depression. Across the studies they pooled, sensitivity was 0.86 and specificity 0.81. In the first eight studies that used ≤50, mean sensitivity was 0.87 and mean specificity 0.76. — [WHO sheet](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/mental-health/who-5_english-original4da539d6ed4b49389e3afe47cda2326a.pdf); [Topp et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1159/000376585)
- Integer arithmetic makes “below 50 / raw below 13” and “50 or below / raw 12 or below” the same rule. Raw 12 × 4 = 48. Raw 13 × 4 = 52. A percentage of exactly 50 is not an integer raw score. — inferred from the scoring rule in both sources above
- A more restrictive cut of ≤28 is described as the level of well-being among patients with DSM-IV major depression (Löwe et al., as summarised by Topp). Despite that stricter cut, sensitivity was 0.93 and specificity 0.83 in that study. Topp also says a baseline mean ≤28 is representative of major depression in primary care (Guico-Pabia pooled desvenlafaxine trials), and that those treated patients only reached a mean of about 50, still short of the population norm. An HAM-D of 18 (treatment-level major depression) corresponded to WHO-5 of 20, and HAM-D of 13 (minor depression) to WHO-5 of 32 (Krieger et al., as summarised by Topp). — [Topp et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1159/000376585)
- Danish general-population studies cited by Topp have a mean WHO-5 of 70 (percentage score). Topp’s treatment goal for trials is to reach the general-population mean. — [Topp et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1159/000376585)
- German adults, representative sample, June–October 2021, N = 2,515, ages 16–101, response rate 42.6%: raw mean 16.89 (SD 5.74). Item-difficulty for the total score Pt = 67.56, which is the percentage-score mean (16.89 × 4). Distribution is negatively skewed (skew −0.90). — [Kliem et al., Front Psychol 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1592614)
- Same German percentile table (percentage scores, cumulative percent at or below the listed score): score 48, 21.3% of all adults (95% CI 19.7–22.9) and 15.6% of ages 16–24 (11.1–20.4); score 28, 9.5% of all adults (8.4–10.7) and 4.0% of ages 16–24. Score 72, 49.5% of all adults, so the all-age median is just above 72, above the mean of 67.6 because of the left skew. — [Kliem et al. 2025, Table 2](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1592614/full)
- Topp states that a difference of about 3 percentage points can be statistically significant but not clinically significant, and that the threshold for a clinically relevant change is considered to be 10 points on the WHO-5 (their citation [27]). A 2025 disease-area review says there is no clear minimal clinically important difference. It traces the working figure to a 2.5-point difference on the raw 0–25 scale (10 points on 0–100) judged clinically relevant against depressive-symptom improvement in desvenlafaxine trial data, and says that, without a robust MCID, the better goal is to restore well-being to the general-population level. — [Topp et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1159/000376585); [Adv Ther 2025;42:3657–3677](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12325-025-03266-9)
- Earlier German norms exist (Brähler, Mühlan, Albani and Schmidt, Diagnostica 2007;53:83–96, doi 10.1026/0012-1924.53.2.83) but the means were not extracted here. Kliem cites Bech, Olsen, Kjoller and Rasmussen, Int J Methods Psychiatr Res 2003, for scoring. — [Kliem et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1592614)

### Inferences
- For Mindhelper (Danish youth) and Nebolus (German pupils and students), a percentage score around 68–70 is ordinary adult well-being. A score at or below 50 is the screening band for poor well-being, and in the 2021 German adult sample that band (≤48) covered about the lowest fifth, not a rare extreme. A score around 28 or below is in the range Topp treats as major-depression level (about the lowest tenth of German adults in 2021).
- Report raw and percentage scores separately. A raw mean of 15 is a percentage of 60, not a low score.

### Gaps
- No Danish youth (13–20) mean and no German pupil or university-student mean were retrieved. The German 16–24 row is a percentile table, not a published mean.
- Topp points to European Quality of Life Survey 2012 country norms in an online supplement. Those country means were not in the fetched article text.
- The 10-point figure is a cited working threshold, not a scale-specific MCID estimated in youth.

## WEMWBS (14-item Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale)

### Takeaway
England and Scotland adult means cluster around 50–52 on a 14–70 scale. The published low-wellbeing band is the bottom 15%, scores 14–42, not a clinical diagnosis cut. Icelandic scores exist in a four-country comparison but the Iceland mean itself was not in the text retrieved. A change of 3 to 8 points is the published range for minimally important individual change.

### Cited Findings
- 14 items, past two weeks, each 1 (none of the time) to 5 (all of the time). Sum 14–70. Higher is better. Covers feeling and functioning. Validated for ages 16 and above. The developers say it is for population monitoring, not for identifying or screening individuals with high or low mental well-being. — [Taggart et al., WEMWBS user guide version 2, 2015](https://measure-wellbeing.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Taggart-et-al-User-Guide-Version-2-2015.pdf); [Warwick scoring guidance](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/innovations/wemwbs/how/)
- Health Survey for England 2011, ages 16+, valid N = 7,020: mean 51.61, SD 8.71, median 53, 25th percentile 47, 75th percentile 57, observed range 14–70. — [Warwick HSE 2011 norms PDF](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/innovations/wemwbs/how/wemwbs_population_norms_in_health_survey_for_england_data_2011.pdf)
- Warwick, citing Tennant et al., Health Qual Life Outcomes 2007;5:63: UK general-population samples, mean 51.0, SD 7. From that, they set low well-being at the bottom 15% (cut 42; expected tail below 42.3) and high well-being at 60 (expected tail above 59.7). — [Warwick scoring guidance](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/innovations/wemwbs/how/); Tennant et al. DOI [10.1186/1477-7525-5-63](https://doi.org/10.1186/1477-7525-5-63) (table not re-opened here; the 51.0 and SD 7 are Warwick’s citation of it)
- User-guide population means (SD, or SE/CI where the guide prints those instead): Scottish validation combined 2006 surveys, mean 50.7 (95% CI 50.3–51.1). Scottish Health Survey ages 16+: about 49.7–50.4 across 2008–2013 (2012 all adults 50.0, SD 8.65; 2013 all adults 50.0, SD 8.65 in the extracted table rows). England Health Survey for England: 2010 all adults 50.9 (SE 0.11); 2011 51.6 (SE 0.1), men 51.8, women 51.4; 2012 52.3 (SE 0.16). — [Taggart et al. 2015 user guide](https://measure-wellbeing.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Taggart-et-al-User-Guide-Version-2-2015.pdf)
- Categorical cuts used on English data: bottom 15% scores 14–42 (low), middle 43–59, top 15% scores 60–70 (high). Ng Fat et al. used a near-identical split with the high band starting at 61 (low 14–42, medium 43–60, high 61–70). — [Stranges et al., BMJ Open 2014;4:e005878](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/4/9/e005878); [Ng Fat et al., Qual Life Res 2017;26:1129–1144](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-016-1454-8)
- Scottish school pupils, 2010 Scottish Schools Adolescent Lifestyle and Substance Use Survey, WEMWBS not the short form: S2 all 50.4 (95% CI 50.3–50.6), boys 50.9, girls 49.9; S4 all 49.7 (49.5–49.8), boys 50.8, girls 48.5. — [Taggart et al. 2015 user guide](https://measure-wellbeing.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Taggart-et-al-User-Guide-Version-2-2015.pdf)
- Denmark, nationally weighted electronic survey, N = 3,508, ages 16–95: WEMWBS mean 52.2 (SD 8.7); men 52.6 (8.7), women 51.9 (8.7). Rank order of means in that paper: Catalonia highest, then Denmark, then Iceland, then England. The Iceland numeric mean was not in the fetched text. Sex differences were small and in the same direction in all four settings. — [Koushede et al., Psychiatry Research 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2018.12.003)
- Individual-level change: Warwick says different methods give a minimum of 3 points and a maximum of 8 points on the 14-item scale, citing Maheswaran, Weich, Powell and Stewart-Brown, Health Qual Life Outcomes 2012;10:156. That paper reports SEM 2.4–3.1 and uses a threshold of 2.77 SEM to flag important individual improvement. The scale was not designed for individual clinical monitoring. — [Warwick scoring guidance](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/innovations/wemwbs/how/); [Maheswaran et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1186/1477-7525-10-156)

### Inferences
- For HappApp (Icelandic general/adult audience), a score around 51–52 is typical of UK and Danish adults. Iceland ranked between Denmark (52.2) and England (about 51–52 in the same era), so an Icelandic population mean in the low 50s is plausible, but that is a rank inference, not a published Icelandic mean. A score of 42 or below is the usual “low wellbeing” band (bottom ~15% in England). It is not a depression diagnosis.
- The Tennant SD of 7 and the HSE 2011 SD of 8.71 are different samples. Use 51.6 (SD 8.7) when the comparator is Health Survey for England 2011, and do not mix the two SDs.

### Gaps
- The Iceland mean and SD from Koushede et al. Table 6 were not in the fetched text. No other Icelandic or Nordic population mean, other than the Danish 52.2, was retrieved.
- No depression or primary-care clinical mean for the 14-item scale was retrieved.
- The exact student mean from the original Warwick/Edinburgh validation samples (Tennant et al. 2007) was not re-extracted. The user guide confirms those student samples existed (scree plot n = 348) but the mean was not in the extracted pages.

## SWEMWBS (7-item short WEMWBS)

### Takeaway
The short form has seven items and a nominal range of 7–35, but UK norms are Rasch-transformed metric scores, not the raw sum. England metric means are about 23.6 (SD about 3.9). Low well-being is the bottom 15%, metric scores from 7 to about 19.5. Metric scores are not a simple sum and must not be compared with raw sums.

### Cited Findings
- Seven items retained from the 14 after a Rasch analysis (Stewart-Brown et al. 2009, cited by Ng Fat). The retained items emphasise psychological functioning more than feeling states. Raw responses are still 1–5 and a raw sum falls between 7 and 35, but a linear transformation is required before parametric comparisons. Warwick: the short form must be transformed using the published conversion, or scores cannot be compared across studies. — [Ng Fat, Scholes, Boniface, Mindell and Stewart-Brown, Qual Life Res 2017;26:1129–1144](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-016-1454-8); [Warwick scoring guidance](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/innovations/wemwbs/how/); [Koushede et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2018.12.003)
- Health Survey for England 2011, transformed SWEMWBS, valid N = 7,196: mean 23.61, SD 3.90, median 23.21, 25th percentile 21.54, 75th percentile 26.02, observed range 7–35. The non-integer percentiles are the evidence that these are metric scores. — [Warwick HSE 2011 norms PDF](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/innovations/wemwbs/how/wemwbs_population_norms_in_health_survey_for_england_data_2011.pdf)
- Health Survey for England 2010–2013, transformed scores, adults 16+ (Ng Fat et al.): men N = 11,948, mean 23.67 (SD 3.92); women N = 15,221, mean 23.59 (SD 3.99). Sex difference was negligible (effect size 0.03, p = 0.100). Ages 16–24: men mean 23.57 (SD 3.61, N = 1,143); women mean 23.17 (SD 3.86, N = 1,540). Warwick, citing this paper, rounds the population figures to mean 23.5 and SD 3.9. — [Ng Fat et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5376387/)
- Low well-being, bottom ~15%: Warwick sets the cut at 19.5 (expected tail below 19.6) and high well-being at 27.5 (expected tail above 27.4). Their stated ranges: low 7.0–19.5, high 27.5–35.0. Ng Fat’s analysis categories were low 7–19.3, medium 20.0–27.0, high 28.1–35, corresponding to about 15% / 71% / 14%. — [Warwick scoring guidance](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/innovations/wemwbs/how/); [Ng Fat et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-016-1454-8)
- Men reporting “very bad” self-rated health had a metric mean of 19.32 (SD 4.08); “very good” health was 24.7 (effect size −1.52). That is a poor-general-health comparison, not a depression diagnosis mean. — [Ng Fat et al. 2017, Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5376387/)
- Wales, school-age adolescents, HBSC/SHRN 2017/18, N = 94,476: the report says scores range from 7 to 35 and the mean was 23.94 (SD 5.38). It does not say the Rasch conversion was applied. An SD of 5.38 is much wider than the English metric SD of about 3.9, so this figure should be treated as a raw sum unless a methods note says otherwise. — [SHRN/HBSC Wales 2017/18 report](https://shrn.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/SHRN-HBSC-NR_31.05.2019.pdf)
- Denmark (Koushede et al. 2018): the fetched text reports a total SWEMWBS mean of 26.4 (SD 4.31) and, separately, sex-specific means of 24.3 (SD 3.81) for men and 24.1 (SD 3.93) for women. Those cannot be the same metric. The sex SDs match English metric SDs; 26.4 with SD 4.31 looks more like an untransformed sum. The paper says final short-form scores were transformed. Do not compare 26.4 with the English metric mean of 23.6. — [Koushede et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2018.12.003)
- Individual change: Warwick says methods give a minimum of 1 point and a maximum of 3 points on SWEMWBS, citing Shah, Cader, Andrews, Wijesekera and Stewart-Brown, Health Qual Life Outcomes 2018;16:239. That paper was not opened here; the 1-to-3 range is Warwick’s summary of it. — [Warwick scoring guidance](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/innovations/wemwbs/how/)

### Inferences
- For Mindhelper (Danish youth), compare a reported score with the English metric mean of about 23.2–23.6 only after confirming the score is the transformed metric. A raw sum in the mid-20s can look “above average” next to a metric norm and still be ordinary. A metric score of 19.5 or below is the bottom-15% low-wellbeing band.
- English 16–24 metric means (23.2 women, 23.6 men) are almost the same as the all-adult metric mean. Youth is not a large shift on this scale in England.

### Gaps
- The conversion table itself (raw sum to metric) was not copied. Only the rule was confirmed: metric ≠ raw sum.
- No Danish youth metric mean was retrieved. The Welsh adolescent mean is probably raw.
- No depression-sample metric mean was retrieved. The “very bad health” mean of 19.3 is the closest low-scoring group in the English norms paper.
- Shah et al. 2018 was not read in full, so the 1–3 point change range rests on Warwick’s citation.

## EQ-VAS (EQ-5D visual analogue scale) and the EQ-5D index

### Takeaway
A Norwegian general-population EQ-VAS mean of 77.9 (SD 18.3) supports the claim that 78 is the population norm. A baseline of 57 matches published Norwegian depression-and-anxiety means (about 56), not the general population. The EQ-VAS is overall health today, not a mental-health scale. QALYs use the EQ-5D index; the Norwegian adult index norm paired with that VAS figure is 0.805, scored with the UK crosswalk, not a Norwegian tariff.

### Cited Findings
- EQ-VAS: vertical scale, “your own health today”, 0 = worst imaginable health, 100 = best imaginable health. It is collected with the EQ-5D descriptive system but is not the index used for QALYs. — [Garratt, Hansen, Augestad, Rand and Stavem, Qual Life Res 2022;31:517–526](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-021-02938-7)
- Norway, EQ-5D-5L postal survey of adults 18+, weighted to age, sex and education. Usable descriptive responses N = 3,120; 73 fewer usable EQ-VAS responses. Weighted EQ-VAS mean 77.9 (SD 18.3) for all respondents, and 77.9 (SD 17.8) for men. Weighted EQ-5D index, UK crosswalk because Norway had no 5L value set at the time: 0.805 (SD 0.201) overall; men 0.814 (SD 0.204). Women had a slightly lower index and a similar VAS. 32% reported no problems on the five dimensions. Survey year weights use Statistics Norway data for 1 October 2019. — [Garratt et al. 2022, Table 6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8284681/)
- The paper states there was no Norwegian EQ-5D-5L value set yet, and that index norms used the UK 3L value set mapped onto 5L descriptions, following the Norwegian Medicines Agency recommendation at the time. A later Norwegian 5L value set exists (hybrid cTTO/DCE model, lowest value −0.453; Qual Life Res paper doi [10.1007/s11136-024-03837-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-024-03837-3)). Population norms rescored with that tariff were not found. — [Garratt et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-021-02938-7)
- Age-standardised general-population EQ-VAS from older EQ-5D-3L national surveys (not Norway): Denmark 83.3, Sweden 82.5, United Kingdom 82.8, Netherlands 81.4, Germany 77.2, Italy 76.9. Range across 20 countries 70.4–83.3. These are 3L-era surveys and are not interchangeable with the Norwegian 5L postal mean, but they show 78 is inside the north-west European band and on the low side of the Nordic 3L figures. — [Janssen, Szende, Cabases, Ramos-Goñi, Vilagut and König, Eur J Health Econ 2019;20:205–216](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10198-018-0955-5)
- Norway, adults on or at risk of sick leave because of depression or anxiety, outpatient clinic, N = 890, EQ-5D-5L with UK crosswalk: index 0.631 (SD 0.187). EQ-VAS is given as 55.7 in the results narrative and as 55.6 (SD 17.7) in the discussion. The authors explicitly compare this with Garratt’s population figures of index 0.805 and VAS 77.9. Depression and anxiety diagnoses together accounted for most of the sample (depression disorders 46%, anxiety disorders 36%, plus related adjustment and mixed diagnoses). — [Sandin et al., Front Psychol 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.655151)
- The same Sandin paper cites Reme et al. 2015: Norwegian patients with common mental disorders and work impairment, EQ-5D-3L, mean EQ-VAS 65.6. Reme was not opened. — [Sandin et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.655151/full)
- Germany, primary-care patients who screened positive for an ICD-10 anxiety disorder, N = 389 (Leipzig, 2005–2006): mean EQ-VAS 63.8 (median 70); mean EQ-5D index 0.66 (median 0.73). 88.4% reported a problem on at least one dimension. A shift toward less anxiety was associated with an index gain of 0.07; a shift toward more anxiety with an index drop of 0.15. The index was more responsive than the VAS. The VAS meaningful-difference coefficient was in a supplement and was not extracted. — [König et al., Health Qual Life Outcomes 2010;8:47](https://doi.org/10.1186/1477-7525-8-47)

### Inferences
- Tankevirus’s statement that the general-population norm is 78 matches Garratt’s Norwegian weighted mean of 77.9, rounded. A baseline EQ-VAS of 57 is about 21 points below that norm and is in line with Sandin’s Norwegian depression/anxiety sick-leave mean of about 56. It is lower than König’s German primary-care anxiety mean of 63.8 and lower than the Reme common-mental-disorder figure of 65.6 cited by Sandin.
- If Tankevirus converts change into QALYs, the relevant population anchor is an index near 0.81 (UK crosswalk on Norwegian adults), not the VAS of 78. An index around 0.63 is the Norwegian clinical comparison. Do not treat 0.805 as a Norwegian-tariff utility.

### Gaps
- No single agreed EQ-VAS minimal important change was retrieved. The 0.07 / −0.15 figures are index changes in German anxiety patients, anchored to a shift on the Beck Anxiety Inventory, not an EQ-VAS MIC.
- Age-specific Norwegian EQ-VAS means are in Garratt Table 6 but were not copied beyond the all-adult 77.9. Stavem et al. 2018 Norwegian EQ-5D-3L norms were not opened.
- Population index norms using the 2024/2025 Norwegian value set were not found.

## MHQoL (Mental Health Quality of Life Questionnaire)

### Takeaway
Seven items, each 0–3, sum 0–21, higher better, plus a separate 0–10 psychological well-being VAS. The only Dutch general-population mean retrieved is 15.5 (SD 2.9) in a small panel sample, against 11.5 (SD 4.0) in mental-healthcare users. No normative bands, no student mean, and no minimal important change were found.

### Cited Findings
- Developed in Dutch for people with subclinical and clinical mental-health problems and for mental-health services. Two parts: MHQoL-7D and MHQoL-VAS. Seven dimensions in the development paper: self-image, independence, mood, relationships, daily activities, physical health, future. Four levels, for example very satisfied = 3 to very dissatisfied = 0. Sum 0–21. Higher is better. The VAS is 0 (“worst imaginable psychological well-being”) to 10 (“best imaginable psychological well-being”). — [van Krugten, Busschbach, Versteegh, Hakkaart-van Roijen and Brouwer, Qual Life Res 2022;31:633–643](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-021-02935-w)
- A later seven-country paper uses the same 0–21 sum and renames the seventh dimension “hope” rather than “future”. Preference-based tariffs were not available at that time; the sum score is the interim scoring. Tariffs were expected for the Netherlands. The instrument is held by Erasmus School of Health Policy & Management and is distributed at https://www.imta.nl/mhqol. — [Enzing, van Krugten, Sabat, Neumann-Böhme, Boer, Knies and Brouwer, Health Qual Life Outcomes 2022;20:128](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12955-022-02041-6)
- Dutch online panel, September 2018. Mental-healthcare users, N = 479 (visited a health professional for mental-health problems in the past 12 months; depression 64.5%, dysthymia 41.8%, anxiety disorder 42.0%): MHQoL-7D mean 11.5 (SD 4.0), VAS mean 5.7 (SD 2.0). General-population subsample, N = 110, quota-matched to age, sex and education: MHQoL-7D mean 15.5 (SD 2.9), VAS mean 7.5 (SD 1.5). The general-population cell is small, and 20 of those 110 had themselves visited a professional for mental-health problems in the past year. Mean age of the general-population cell 48.6 (SD 16.1). — [van Krugten et al. 2022, Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8847188/)
- Seven European countries (Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, Portugal, the Netherlands, United Kingdom), online panels, 5–16 November 2020, COVID-policy survey, N about 7,115: mean sum 14.1 (SD 3.8), mean VAS 6.6 (SD 2.2). The authors say this is below the Dutch general-population figures of 15.5 and 7.5. Country-by-age means ranged from 12.3 in UK adults aged 18–24 to 15.9 in Danish adults aged 65+. The Netherlands-only mean was in Table 3 and was not extracted. Dimension means (0–3) ranged from 1.8 (hope) to 2.2 (physical health). Scores rose with age; the youngest group was worse than the oldest on every dimension except physical health. These are COVID-period general-public scores, not pre-pandemic norms and not student norms. — [Enzing et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12955-022-02041-6)

### Inferences
- For GetStarted (Dutch university students), 15–16 on the 0–21 sum is the only published Dutch “general public” anchor, and it is a small, mostly middle-aged panel, not students. A score around 11–12 resembles the Dutch mental-healthcare users (11.5) or the lowest young-adult cell in the 2020 European survey (UK 18–24, 12.3). There are no published bands such as low / average / high.
- Do not read the 0–10 VAS as an EQ-VAS. A 7.5 on the MHQoL-VAS is the Dutch panel mean for psychological well-being, on a 0–10 scale.

### Gaps
- No university-student mean. No Dutch young-adult mean extracted from the seven-country table. No minimal important change. No official normative bands. The general-population mean rests on N = 110. Tariffs may now exist; none were retrieved, and the 2022 paper said they were not yet available.

## SF-12 (12-item Short Form Health Survey)

### Takeaway
PCS and MCS are norm-based summaries anchored to the US general population at mean 50, SD 10. Higher is better. European general-population MCS scores sit a little above that anchor (about 53–54). Outpatients starting or switching an antidepressant in a five-country European cohort, including Germany, had a mean MCS of 26.5 (SD 9.2). A European screening cut for current depressive disorder is about 45.6, which is not the same thing as the mean among people who have depression.

### Cited Findings
- 12 items, shorter form of the SF-36, built to reproduce the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS). Standard scoring uses US-derived item weights. In large US population surveys both PCS-12 and MCS-12 have mean 50 and SD 10. A score of 50 is the typical US person; each point is one-tenth of a standard deviation. Scoring here follows Ware, Kosinski and Keller’s manual; the NLSY note says it uses version 1, not version 2, and that the formula is not public. — [NLSY79 SF-12 scoring note](https://www.nlsinfo.org/content/cohorts/nlsy79/other-documentation/codebook-supplement/nlsy79-appendix-19-sf-12-health-scale); Ware, Kosinski and Keller, Med Care 1996;34:220–233, as cited by Saragoussi et al.
- Because the 50 is built into US weights, a European country mean need not be 50. ESEMeD, representative non-institutionalised adults in Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands and Spain, N = 21,425, SF-12v1, 4-week recall on most items: overall MCS-12 mean 53.7. France was lowest at 52.6. Belgium, Germany and the Netherlands were highest, near 54.4. — [Vilagut et al., Value Health 2013;16:564–573](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2013.01.006)
- Same paper, best Youden cut for 30-day DSM-IV depressive disorder (major depressive episode or dysthymia) on MCS-12: 45.6 (sensitivity 0.86, specificity 0.88). For 12-month depressive disorder the cut was 48.9 (sensitivity 0.74, specificity 0.83). The authors note that 45.6 is higher than the US SF-36 MCS cut of 42 or below for depressive disorders, and that both cuts sit about 8 points below their own population mean (53.7 in this European sample, 50 in the US). Germany’s AUC for 12-month depressive disorders was about 0.89. This cut is a screening threshold in the general population. It is not the mean MCS of people with depression. — [Vilagut et al. 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1098301513000168)
- PERFORM cohort: 1,159 outpatients aged 18–65 with major depressive disorder in France, Germany, Spain, Sweden and the UK, at the start of antidepressant monotherapy or a first switch. Baseline MCS 26.5 (SD 9.2); PCS 45.2 (SD 12.1). At 12 months MCS 38.7 (SD 11.6); PCS 45.3 (SD 11.1). The authors treat MCS below 42.0, citing Ware, as significant mental-health impairment, and say the month-12 mean of 38.7 was still below that threshold. PCS stayed near the population range, so the depression deficit is on MCS. — [Saragoussi, Christensen, Hammer-Helmich, Rive, Touya and Haro, Neuropsychiatr Dis Treat 2018;14:1339–1350](https://doi.org/10.2147/NDT.S159276)
- German SF-12 version 2 population norms have been published (Diagnostica paper doi [10.1026/0012-1924/a000205](https://doi.org/10.1026/0012-1924/a000205)) but the means were not extracted. iFightDepression (Oehler et al. 2020) was not re-analysed here; only external benchmarks were collected.

### Inferences
- For the German iFightDepression RCT, read MCS against two different anchors. About 50–54 is general-population mental-health-related quality of life (US scoring anchor 50, European survey mean about 54, Germany near 54). About 26–27 is a treated major-depression outpatient mean at the point of starting or switching medication. A score below 42 is the impairment threshold Saragoussi cites; Vilagut’s 45.6 is a sensitive general-population screen for a current depressive disorder, so many people under 45.6 will not have a diagnosis, and people with a diagnosis average far lower than 45.6.
- PCS near 45 in depression is only a small step below the US anchor of 50. A low PCS should not be read as the depression signal; MCS is.

### Gaps
- No Germany-only MCS mean inside the PERFORM depression cohort was extracted (26.5 is the five-country pool).
- No minimal important change for SF-12 MCS was retrieved. The 42-point impairment line is a threshold, not an MIC.
- Version 1 and version 2 use different scoring. The ESEMeD and the NLSY description are version 1. Which version Oehler et al. 2020 used was not checked.

## Subjective Happiness Scale (SHS)

### Takeaway
Four items, scored as a 1–7 mean (equivalently a 4–28 sum). US college means fall around 4.6–5.1; one US community-adult mean is 5.62. There is no clinical cut-off in the original paper. An Italian community norming study exists, but its mean was not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Lyubomirsky and Lepper, Social Indicators Research 1999;46:137–155: 4 items, each a 7-point scale. The fourth item is reverse-coded. The composite is the average of the four items. Range 1.0–7.0. Higher is greater happiness. A sum of 4–28 is the same information divided by 4; the paper itself reports the mean, not the sum. Fourteen samples, N = 2,732. DOI [10.1023/A:1006824100041](https://doi.org/10.1023/a:1006824100041). — [Lyubomirsky and Lepper 1999 PDF](https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Lyubomirsky-Lepper-1999.pdf)
- Table I means (SD), selected rows: US public-college sample 1, N = 551, 4.89 (1.11); US public-college sample 2, N = 372, 4.99 (1.07); US public-college sample 3, N = 156, 5.07 (1.14); US private-college samples from 4.63 (1.72) to 4.96 (1.19); Russian college, N = 67, 4.84 (1.13); US high-school, N = 36, 5.13 (0.98); US city community adults, N = 198, 5.62 (0.96); US retired community, 5.62 (0.96); US adult women in a town sample, 4.80 (1.12); Russian city community adults, 4.02 (0.93). College respondents’ mean age was 19.2. The paper does not define a clinical cut-off, a low-happiness band, or a minimal important change. — [Lyubomirsky and Lepper 1999](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2185n8gb/qt2185n8gb.pdf)
- Italian translation and norming: community sample N = 993, ages 18–85, age and gender stratified to the population pyramid, administered with life-satisfaction items and anxiety and depression scales. Unidimensional. The paper says it provides normative data. The mean and SD were in a table that was not in the fetched text. — [Iani, Lauriola, Layous and Sirigatti, Soc Indic Res 2014;118:953–967](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11205-013-0468-7)

### Inferences
- For Bene in Mente (Italian university students), a 1–7 mean around 4.8–5.1 matches the original college samples, including a small Russian student sample (4.84). A mean around 5.6 matches the higher US community and retired samples and should not be treated as the student benchmark. The Russian community mean of 4.02 shows cultural samples can sit far from the US college cluster; it is not a clinical cut.
- If a paper reports a total of 20, that is an item mean of 5.0. If it reports 4.9, do not compare it with a 4–28 total.

### Gaps
- Italian community mean and SD (Iani et al. Table 3) were not retrieved. No Italian university-student mean was found. No clinical or low-happiness cut-off and no minimal important change were found. The original paper does not provide one.

## Psychological Well-Being Scale (Ryff PWB)

### Takeaway
The form most often used in brief surveys is the 18-item version (3 items on each of 6 dimensions), not the 42-, 84- or 120-item forms. A mean from one version, one response scale, or one scoring method (item mean versus subscale sum versus total sum) does not transfer to the others. No Italian student mean, no clinical cut-off, and no minimal important change were retrieved. The Bene in Mente version is not identified in the submission, so no single benchmark should be applied.

### Cited Findings
- Ryff’s model has six dimensions: self-acceptance, positive relations with others, autonomy, environmental mastery, purpose in life, and personal growth. Higher is better after negatively worded items are reverse-scored. The parent item pool is 20 items per dimension (120 items). Shorter forms in use include 84 (14 per dimension), 54, 42 (7 per dimension) and 18 (3 per dimension). — [Garcia et al. on the Swedish 18-item version, Front Psychol 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10580072/); [Ryff and Keyes, J Pers Soc Psychol 1995;69:719–727](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.69.4.719)
- The 18-item form takes three items from each dimension (Ryff and Keyes 1995). Subscale correlations with the 120-item parent scales were 0.70–0.89 in that paper, as cited by the Swedish study. MIDUS I and II administered 18 items by self-administered questionnaire to US adults aged 25–74. MIDUS I used a 7-point scale; the midpoint label changed between waves. SPARQ’s 18-item scoring instructions: 7-point agree–disagree scale, reverse-score specified items, then sum the three items of each subscale. Higher subscale sums mean higher well-being. On that 7-point scoring, each subscale runs from 3 to 21. An 18-item total sum would run from 18 to 126. Some users instead report the mean of the 18 items on the 1–7 scale. — [SPARQ 18-item sheet, citing Ryff and Keyes 1995 and MIDUS II documentation](https://datashare.ed.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10283/8553/Ryff-18.pdf); [Springer, Pudrovska and Hauser, PMC3014614](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3014614/)
- A Swedish 18-item version used a 6-point scale (1 strongly disagree to 6 strongly agree), so its total runs from 18 to 108, not 18 to 126. In a volunteer sample aged 13–76 (N = 768, mean age 25.2), subscale internal consistency was modest to poor for several dimensions (purpose in life Cronbach’s alpha 0.08 in that study; autonomy also 0.48). The six-factor model was not clearly superior to a five-factor model that dropped purpose in life. This is a psychometric warning about the 18-item form, not a population mean. — [Garcia et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10580072/)
- The 42-item form is the medium length often used when each subscale needs more than three items (7 items × 6). SPARQ describes it as a 6–8 minute scale versus 3–5 minutes for 18 items, and says both have been used with US adults. It does not publish one mean that covers both. — [SPARQ Psychological Wellbeing Scale page](https://sparqtools.org/mobility-measure/psychological-wellbeing-scale/)
- Italian 18-item factor studies are cited in the Swedish review (Sirigatti et al. 2009) as supporting the six-factor model. No Italian means were in the fetched text. A Romanian paper that came up in search validated the 84-item long form, not the 18-item form, and its means were not used. — [Garcia et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10580072/)

### Inferences
- For a brief student survey, the 18-item Ryff and Keyes form is the default assumption, with the 42-item form the next most likely. Until Bene in Mente states the item count, the response scale (6-point or 7-point), and whether they report subscale sums, a total sum, or item means, a published mean from another study would misclassify the sample.
- Even within the 18-item form, an item-mean of 4.5 on a 1–7 scale is not comparable to a subscale sum of 4.5 or to a total of 4.5. Three items per subscale also means subscale scores are coarse; a one-point subscale change can be one item moving by one step.

### Gaps
- No primary-source table of MIDUS or European subscale means was retrieved, so no numeric “normal” score is reported here on purpose.
- No Italian university-student means. No clinical cut-off. No minimal important change. No confirmation of which form Bene in Mente used (84, 42, 18 or 9 items).
