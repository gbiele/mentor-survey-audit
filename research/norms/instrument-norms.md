# Published norms fit few survey scores

The JA-MENTOR youth survey can be set against a published distribution on its own scoring for a short list of columns: the Brief Child and Family Phone Interview attention sum, the KIDSCREEN-10 raw sum, one CYRM-12 school mean, the Child and Adolescent Trauma Screen symptom total, and the factor rules for the German social-media and gaming scales. **Attention is elevated above 7** on the 0–12 youth brief screen for ages 6–18, and that is a threshold, not a community mean; no adolescent self-report mean on that sum was found. **European adolescents aged 12–18 average 28.8 out of 40** on KIDSCREEN-10 once the manual’s linear rescaling is reversed, while the T-scores in the KIDSCREEN papers are a Rasch score the survey does not compute. **Turkish students aged 10–14 average 37.47 out of 48** on the CYRM-12, and the scale’s own centre publishes no low-resilience cut. Both WHO ACE-IQ totals (binary and frequency, each 0–13) match the survey’s category rules closely enough to be the right instrument, and **no European adolescent distribution of either total was found**. Among the other optional modules, mental-health literacy, the Digital Addiction Scale for Children, the Columbia suicide screener, and the MoBa sleep questions do not supply a youth normal score on the metric the survey stores.

## Attention is the only BCFPI sum with a matching raw flag

The survey scores symptom items as never = 0, sometimes = 1, and often = 2, so each six-item sum runs from 0 to 12 and each functioning domain, four items, runs from 0 to 8. Higher means more difficulty. The 2009 youth brief screen, completed by youth aged 12 or over, uses those anchors and prints integer flags described as elevated relative to normed values for **6- to 18-year-olds**, with no split by sex and no statement that the flag equals a T-score of 70 ([BCFPI youth brief screen, 2009](https://www.shared-care.ca/files/Youth_Questionnaire_Final.pdf)). **Regulation of attention matches that form item for item**: easily distracted, failing to finish, jumping between activities, difficulty following instructions, impulsivity, and fidgeting, the same six that load the factor in the inpatient paper and in the Lakehead thesis ([Cook et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3647631/); [Bova, 2006](https://knowledgecommons.lakeheadu.ca/bitstreams/bf84d1ad-40d4-41b4-a69f-644d12b32d0b/download)). On that sum, **a score above 7 is the elevated range** — 8, 9, 10, 11, or 12. A sum of 7 is not above the line. The sheet pools ages 6–18, so the flag is coarser than the manual, which builds separate norms for ages 6–12 and 13–18 and for boys and girls ([Bova, 2006](https://knowledgecommons.lakeheadu.ca/bitstreams/bf84d1ad-40d4-41b4-a69f-644d12b32d0b/download); [Barwick, Boydell, Cunningham, and Ferguson, 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2538706/)).

The community mean on that attention sum was not printed for adolescent self-report. The only Ontario community raw means located are parent report in a mixed-age norm file whose participants, taken together, had a mean age of **10.96 years (SD 3.59)**. In the non-First Nations general-population column, attention averages **3.20 (SD 2.69)** on the six-item 0–12 metric; the clinical parent column in the same file averages **6.41 (SD 3.27)** ([Bova, 2006, Tables 4 and 5](https://knowledgecommons.lakeheadu.ca/bitstreams/bf84d1ad-40d4-41b4-a69f-644d12b32d0b/download)). A Swedish study of new child-and-adolescent mental-health referrals, again parent interview, ages 13–17, n = 113, prints an ADHD raw mean of **6.54 (SD 3.07)** ([Högberg et al., 2018](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.21307/sjcapp-2018-009.pdf)). Those figures show where parent-reported attention sits, in a community file centred near age 11 and in two treated samples. They are not youth self-report norms for ages 12–18, and the thesis does not split the community mean into the manual’s age bands. The kappa cuts in the Swedish table, including ADHD at 7, were chosen so the screen-positive rate matched a clinic diagnosis. They are not the brief-screen rule and not a conversion of T = 70.

Cooperativeness, managing anxiety, and managing mood each differ from the published six by one item, so the printed flags belong to a different sum. The brief screen flags **cooperativeness above 7, managing anxiety above 6, and managing mood above 5**, again for ages 6–18 and both sexes ([BCFPI youth brief screen, 2009](https://www.shared-care.ca/files/Youth_Questionnaire_Final.pdf)). The published cooperativeness items include “easily annoyed by others”; the survey list puts “lose temper” in that place. Managing anxiety includes “overly anxious to please people”; the survey list puts “anxious or nervous” there. Managing mood includes “trouble enjoying yourself”; the survey list puts “lonely” there. The same six-item lists appear in Cook and colleagues’ factor table and in Bova’s item table ([Cook et al., 2013, Table 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3647631/); [Bova, 2006, Table 1](https://knowledgecommons.lakeheadu.ca/bitstreams/bf84d1ad-40d4-41b4-a69f-644d12b32d0b/download)). Bova’s community mood mean of **1.59 (SD 2.02)** is a further mismatch: it is a nine-item sum that adds weight loss, talk of killing oneself, and deliberate self-harm to the six mood items. Her community cooperativeness mean of **3.24 (SD 2.53)** and managing-anxiety mean of **2.99 (SD 2.27)** are on the published six, parent report, mixed ages. The Swedish 13–17 parent means for oppositional behaviour (**6.93, SD 3.27**), generalised anxiety (**5.27, SD 3.11**), and depression (**6.19, SD 3.35**) are clinical raw scores on that paper’s six-item scales, not on the survey’s swapped items ([Högberg et al., 2018](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.21307/sjcapp-2018-009.pdf)).

What service papers actually print for these scales is a T-score. Inside each age-by-sex cell the community mean is **50 and the SD is 10** by construction. A T-score of **70** is “significantly elevated” (higher than 98% of the norming population) and a T-score of **65** is the guide’s borderline mark and, in one Ontario utilisation study, the clinical cut at the 93rd percentile ([BCFPI case-report guide](https://bcfpi.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/bcfpi_interpreting_case_report.pdf); [Reid et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-019-4842-2)). No open source printed the raw score that equals either cut for any age-by-sex cell, for parent report or for youth self-report. The survey file does not contain T-scores, and none were back-calculated here. The clinical location that does exist is parent or guardian report at an Ontario tertiary centre: 227 residential admissions, 171 of them boys, ages 6.28–16.74, mean **12.06 (SD 2.46)**. The outpatient column is a 2006 personal communication with no printed N, SD, or age split ([Cook et al., 2013, Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3647631/)).

In that residential group, managing anxiety sits near the community centre of the T metric while attention, cooperativeness, and mood sit in the mid-70s. A survey sum of 8 on attention meets the brief-screen flag. It is not “a T of 74.” The 2009 development and screening papers, the usual citations for the instrument’s structure and for its accuracy against a diagnostic interview, do not supply the missing raw mean: both are closed access, and both abstracts report fit or areas under the curve, not subscale means ([Cunningham, Boyle, Hong, Pettingill, and Bohaychuk, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.2008.01970.x); [Boyle et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.2008.01971.x)).

Three further survey blocks have no applicable BCFPI norm. The published problem scales are attention, cooperativeness, conduct, separation from parents, managing anxiety, and managing mood. Social anxiety is not among them, and the Ontario Child Health Study social-phobia scale that was dropped “to align with the BCFPI” has five items, not these six ([Cunningham et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.2008.01970.x); [Goulter et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jora.12779)). Managing-anxiety flags, raw or T, do not transfer to the social-anxiety column. The emotion block is six of the twelve Affect Regulation Checklist items, three about dysregulation and three about suppression, summed with nothing reversed. The checklist is scored as separate factors, on a 3-point or 5-point “like me” scale, and the survey drops one item from each of those two factors plus the whole reflection factor ([Santos et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10826-025-03044-9); [Goulter et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jora.12779)). A later BCFPI preprint evaluated new affect-regulation scales in 2,647 Canadian parents and printed no raw means ([BCFPI-V3 preprint](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/92vh5_v1)). No norm for this 0–12 sum was found. Functioning names are real — social participation, quality of relationships, and school participation and achievement — and the published subscales are **three items each**, reported as T-scores ([Gordon et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054705283881); [Bova, 2006](https://knowledgecommons.lakeheadu.ca/bitstreams/bf84d1ad-40d4-41b4-a69f-644d12b32d0b/download)). The survey’s four-item 0–8 sums, which add screen time, daily routines, and a friends-versus-peers split, have no published raw mean and no raw flag. The 2009 brief screen has no functioning section. The residential and outpatient T means in the table above are the clinical complement on the published three-item scales, parent report, and they are not 0–8 benchmarks.

## No European adolescent ACE-IQ split exists on either total

The survey’s two adversity totals are the WHO 13-category scores, each running from 0 to 13: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, violence against household members, household substance use, household mental illness, household incarceration, parental separation or death, emotional neglect, physical neglect, bullying, community violence, and collective violence. The frequency total follows the guidance’s higher thresholds, including “many times” for physical and emotional abuse, yelling at a household member, physical neglect, bullying, and community violence, and “a few times or many times” for seeing a household member hit ([WHO ACE-IQ guidance](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/documents/child-maltreatment/ace-iq-guidance-for-analysing.pdf)). Household substance use, mental illness, incarceration, and parental separation or death are yes/no on both totals. Sexual abuse and collective violence count at any occurrence on both totals. The survey applies “rarely or never” to the two parent-relationship items on both totals. That is the frequency rule. On the binary total it is the guidance’s “no” only if “no” means rarely or never. Papers that count “sometimes” as binary emotional neglect will run higher than the survey on the same answers. The guidance defines no clinical cut. “Four or more” comes from the original US ACE questionnaire, a shorter household list, and WHO does not adopt it for the 13-category instrument ([WHO ACE-IQ guidance](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/documents/child-maltreatment/ace-iq-guidance-for-analysing.pdf); [Felitti et al., 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0749-3797(98)00017-8)).

No school or general-population adolescent study was found that reports the percent at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 or more on these 13 categories, for either scoring method, in Europe or in the other countries searched. A 2022 review of community ACE-IQ studies concluded that European, American, and Oceanian data were too sparse to describe, that children and adolescents were underrepresented, and that studies often omitted prevalence or means; a later review repeated that European prevalence data were too sparse to pool, and that studies often do not say which scoring method they used ([Pace, Muzi, Rogier, Meinero, and Marcenaro, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2022.105640); [Pace, Muzi, Rogier, Ballestracci, and Mazzola, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/car.2883)). The pooled community slogan in that first review — about 75% with any ACE and a mean of three — mixes methods and regions. The pooled clinical mean of **4.93**, with 81.74% reporting any ACE, does the same. Neither is a norm for either survey total.

The youth study that did use both WHO methods is rural Mchinji District, Malawi, July 2017: **n = 410, ages 10–16, mean 12.99 (SD 1.74)**, 47% female, in a low-income setting with high adult HIV prevalence. On the binary total the mean was **6.2**, the median 6.0, the range 0–12, more than 99% reported at least one category, and nearly 30% reported more than 7. On the frequency total the mean was **2.9** and the median 3.0 ([Kidman, Smith, Piccolo, and Kohler, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6513701/)). The paper does not give the percent at 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 or more. The gap between 6.2 and 2.9 in one sample is the practical warning for this survey: the two columns the file stores will not rank youth in the same way. A later wave of the same cohort, counting a category if it was reported at either interview, gives a binary mean of **6.74** and nearly 90% at 4 or more ([Kidman, Breton, Behrman, Rui, and Kohler, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2024.106895)). That is a two-wave cumulative count, not one administration. It is a high-adversity youth comparison, not a European school benchmark.

The closest European general-population table is adult. A representative Slovak sample in April 2019, **n = 1,018, ages 18–85, mean 46.2 (SD 16.6)**, names the 13 WHO domains. The binary total averages **2.7 (SD 2.6)**: no ACE **22.9%**, one ACE **22.0%**, two or three **23.7%**, four or more **31.4%**. The frequency column averages **0.9 (SD 1.5)**: no ACE **53.7%**, one **26.3%**, two or three **12.6%**, four or more **7.4%** ([Kaščáková, Fürstová, Hašto, and Tavel, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11788736/)). Scores of exactly 2 and exactly 3 are not printed. Binary emotional neglect (30.9%) is far above the frequency column (9.1%), so the binary neglect rule is looser than a confirmed “rarely or never,” and sexual abuse falls from 3.7% to 1.5% even though WHO counts any sexual abuse on both totals. Chilean adults in the MAUCO cohort, n = 651, mean age 48.4, average **5.4 (SD 2.9)** binary and **3.0 (SD 2.7)** frequency, with the same pattern of looser binary neglect ([Vallejos, Sánchez, and Bühring, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/children12030340)). Polish women scored on the binary 0–13 version average **6.46 (SD 2.42)** in an internalising treatment group (n = 79, anxiety or mixed anxiety-depression) and **6.05 (SD 3.22)** in an externalising group (n = 80, addiction, including incarceration), against **2.84 (SD 2.13)** in women without adaptation problems (n = 129, part-time students and employees) ([Gąsior and Chodkiewicz, 2020](https://cipp.ug.edu.pl/pdf-122262-73723?filename=Adverse-childhood-experie.pdf)). Those three adult studies are the published European and community contrasts on a 13-category form. They are not youth norms, and the Slovak and Chilean binary totals are not a clean copy of the survey’s neglect rule. The frequency means — 0.9 in Slovak adults, 3.0 in Chilean adults, 2.9 in Malawian adolescents — are the nearer comparisons for the survey’s frequency column, each with its sample label attached.

Several widely quoted adolescent or student prevalences are different instruments. A Shanghai student paper’s 42.6% binary and 12.2% frequency at four or more uses 12 categories, and its binary neglect rate (2.1%) against a frequency rate of 31.6% reverses the WHO threshold ([Chen, Yu, Wang, and Gross, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42844-022-00076-8)). A German university study that followed the WHO frequency manual dropped collective violence, merged community violence into peer violence, and added serious financial problems ([Wiehn, Hornberg, and Fischer, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6090638/)). Hungarian school students aged 12–17 were given a 10-item intrafamilial questionnaire ([Kovács-Tóth, Oláh, Kuritárné Szabó, and Fekete, 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1161620)). Mexican schooled adolescents were scored on 16 adversities, and US high-school estimates such as 22.4% with four or more come from an eight-item Youth Risk Behavior Survey module ([Casas-Muñoz et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2023.106492); [Swedo et al.](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2024-066633)). None of those percents is a benchmark for either survey total. No child-welfare or adolescent mental-health-clinic sample with both WHO totals on the 13 categories was found.

## European adolescents average 28.8 of 40 on KIDSCREEN-10

The survey’s ten items are the KIDSCREEN-10 index, in the published order: fit and well, full of energy, sad, lonely, time for yourself, free time, parents treated you fairly, fun with friends, got on well at school, and able to pay attention. Each item has five ordered answers about the last week. The manual recodes **“felt sad” and “felt lonely”** so that higher scores mean better health-related quality of life, then sums items scored 1 to 5 into a raw total from **10 to 50** ([Ravens-Sieberer et al., 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2977059/); [KIDSCREEN manual](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/0/module/12416751312/name/KIDSCREEN_manual_En%20glish.pdf)). If the survey’s 0–4 points sit in that same order and the same two items are reversed, **survey sum + 10 is the official raw sum**. Two items use an intensity wording (not at all through extremely) and eight use a frequency wording (never through always). The shift assumes the survey kept that order. It does not produce a T-score.

Official scoring then assigns each raw sum a Rasch person parameter and standardises it to a T-score with mean 50 and SD 10 in the European reference sample. The manual states that this step is non-linear: nearly linear in the middle of the scale and steeper at the extremes ([KIDSCREEN manual](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/0/module/12416751312/name/KIDSCREEN_manual_En%20glish.pdf); [KIDSCREEN Group, scoring by hand](https://www.kidscreen.org/english/analysis/evaluation-by-hand/)). A published anchor is raw 36 = person parameter 0.52 = **international T 43.35**. The survey does not compute that T. A straight rescaling of 0–40 or 10–50 does not recover it. The international T for official raw 35, which is a survey score of 25, was not in the sources, and none was interpolated.

The reference sample is the KIDSCREEN project in 13 European countries. The group-norm appendix, self-report, gives adolescents aged **12–18, sexes combined, n = 14,932**, an international T mean of **48.51 (SD 9.28)** and a 0–100 mean of **71.93 (SD 14.95)**. Girls 12–18 (n = 7,938) sit at T **47.21 (SD 8.98)** and 0–100 **69.72 (SD 15.34)**. Boys 12–18 (n = 6,970) sit at T **49.97 (SD 9.40)** and 0–100 **74.43 (SD 14.09)**. Children aged 8–11 score higher, T **53.90 (SD 10.73)**, n = 5,715. The pooled ages 8–18 sample is fixed at T 50 (SD 10) by construction, n = 20,647, with a 0–100 mean of 74.07 ([European group norms, Appendix A7_A](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/1709645508/module/12417032512/name/A7_A_european_normdata_group-level_comparisons.pdf)). The 2010 validity paper’s adolescent T mean is close, **47.97 (SD 9.16)**, in 22,830 children and adolescents aged 8–18 overall; self-report alpha was 0.82, and 0.81 at ages 12–18 ([Ravens-Sieberer et al., 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2977059/)). The appendix is the norm table. Country means in that paper are for ages 8–18, adjusted for age and gender, and are not adolescent benchmarks.

The manual’s 0–100 column is a linear rescaling of the raw sum: raw 10 = 0, raw 50 = 100, so 0–100 = (raw − 10) / 40 × 100 ([KIDSCREEN manual, Table A2-16](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/0/module/12416751312/name/KIDSCREEN_manual_En%20glish.pdf)). Reversing that identity, the adolescent 0–100 mean of 71.93 corresponds to an official raw mean of **38.8 on the 10–50 scale**, which is **28.8 on the survey’s 0–40 scale**. Girls correspond to **27.9** and boys to **29.8**. Those three raw means are calculated from the printed 0–100 means. They are not printed as 10–50 means in the appendix. The ages 8–18 figure of 74.07, about 29.6 out of 40, includes the younger children who score higher and is the wrong centre for an adolescent sample.

A survey score of **25 out of 40** is official raw 35 and a 0–100 score of 62.5, under the same shift. In the manual’s individual-norm table, raw 35 is the **28.3rd percentile** among European adolescents aged 12–18, the **33.7th** among girls that age, and the **21.9th** among boys ([KIDSCREEN manual, Table A2-16](https://s2f1ad284f5ffc52e.jimcontent.com/download/version/0/module/12416751312/name/KIDSCREEN_manual_En%20glish.pdf)). It sits 3.8 points under the derived adolescent mean of 28.8. The same row prints a within-adolescent T of 44.0, defined for that age band alone. That column is not the international Rasch T used in papers. The nearest published international T is 43.35 at raw 36, so raw 35 lies below that anchor, and the exact international T was not retrieved.

“Low” quality of life, in the authors’ own materials, is a T-score rule. The KIDSCREEN Group says the questionnaire does not separate healthy from ill, and still treats **T below 40** as low and T above 60 as high ([KIDSCREEN analysis FAQ](https://www.kidscreen.org/english/faq/analysis/)). A re-analysis of the German KiGGS survey put the optimal cut for older adolescents’ self-report at **40.29**, and suggested 40, against mental-health problems on the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire; classification was acceptable (areas under the curve 0.77 to 0.79) and cuts against chronic disease were not ([Quality of Life Research, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-019-02324-4)). Neither cut is a raw-sum threshold. A survey score of 25 must not be called “under the low band” until the Rasch table is applied.

The clinical contrast in the European sample is also on the international T, and the age-specific means for ages 12–18 were not printed. For ages 8–18, youth with a self-reported Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire in the abnormal range averaged **T 40.08 (SD 8.37, n = 711)**, against **T 49.74 (SD 9.00, n = 11,537)** in the normal range, a large effect (d = 1.06) that the paper also reports in the 12–18 column. Special health care needs shifted the mean only to **T 47.31 (SD 9.18)** from 50.52, d = 0.32 at ages 12–18 as well as overall ([Ravens-Sieberer et al., 2010, Table 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2977059/)). Those T means were not converted to raw scores. The conversion is non-linear, and the inverse table was not opened. No clinical sample was found that reports the KIDSCREEN-10 as a raw sum on 10–50 or on 0–40.

## A Turkish school mean of 37.47 anchors the CYRM-12

The survey’s twelve statements are the Liebenberg, Ungar, and LeBlanc reduction: people to look up to, education matters, caregivers know a lot about me, finishing what I start, solving problems without harming myself or others, knowing where to get help, belonging at school, family standing by me, friends standing by me, fair treatment in the community, chances to develop useful skills, and enjoying cultural and family traditions ([Liebenberg, Ungar, and LeBlanc, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6974279/)). Items are positively worded. Nothing is reversed. Published five-point forms sum from **12 to 60**. If the survey’s 0–4 anchors are those five ordered points, **survey sum + 12 is the published sum**, and the SD is unchanged by the shift. The Resilience Research Centre now recommends the 17-item CYRM-R. On cut-offs it says it has been asked for a score that means good or normal resilience and does not provide one, because any threshold would vary by context, and that norms are not well established ([CYRM user manual, v2.5](https://resilienceresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/CYRM-ARM-user-manual-V2.5.pdf)). The manual’s alternative — more than one SD above the user’s own sample mean as higher, within one SD as moderate, below one SD as low — is a within-sample description written around the revised measures. It is not a fixed score on the CYRM-12.

The original validation does not fill the gap. Atlantic Canadian youth in multiple services (child welfare, mental health, justice, special education, community programmes), n = 122, mean age 18, were used to choose the items. A school sample of grades 7–12, about **1,494** in the confirmatory analysis and mean age **15**, had an alpha of **0.840** for the twelve items. Neither sample has a published total mean or SD ([Liebenberg et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6974279/)). The Rasch paper that produced the 17-item revision, 408 Canadian youth aged 11–19, likewise prints no 12–60 mean ([Jefferies, McGarrigle, and Ungar](https://doi.org/10.1080/23761407.2018.1548403)). Appendix C of the manual gives quartile bands for one Canadian study of 1,596 English-speaking youth aged 11–20, and those bands assume a maximum of **85**, the 17-item total. They are not CYRM-12 scores and not a clinical cut ([CYRM manual, Appendix C](https://centerofinnovationnm.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/CYRM__ARM-User_Manual.pdf.pdf)).

The school mean that is on the matching 12–60 metric comes from five Turkish secondary schools: **n = 501, ages 10–14**, 51.7% girls. Items run from “does not describe me at all” (1) to “describes me a lot” (5), with no reverse coding, and the resilience mean is **49.47 (SD 8.08)** ([Çıtak, Kanbur, and Kurşuncu, 2023](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1407151.pdf)). On the survey’s 0–48 metric that is **37.47 (SD 8.08)**. Sex-specific means were not in the extracted text. This is a Turkish school sample, not a Western European general-population norm. No Norwegian, German, British, Spanish, or Dutch school mean on these twelve items scored 1–5 was found. A European study of 1,607 immigrant and refugee adolescents, mean age 15.3, in Belgium, Denmark, Finland, Norway, Sweden, and the United Kingdom names the CYRM-12 and then prints an example item, “Do you feel you fit in with other peers?”, that is not one of the Liebenberg statements, and it reports standardised profile scores rather than a 12–60 mean ([Aalto et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11951332/)).

A Slovenian online panel of young adults, n = 330, ages 18–24, mean 21.2, reports a mean item score of **3.76 (SD 0.68)** on a five-point form whose twelfth item reads “I enjoy my community’s traditions” ([Frontiers in Psychology, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1467174)). If 3.76 is the person-level mean of the twelve items, the 12–60 sum mean is 45.12 (SD 8.16) and the survey metric is 33.12 (SD 8.16). Both the multiplication and the wording of item 12 are assumptions, and the sample is older than a school survey. Iranian students aged 14–18, n = 440, on the 1–5 CYRM-12 showed no sex difference, t(438) = −0.04, and the total mean was not in the text retrieved ([Ghahremani et al.](https://brieflands.com/journals/ijpbs/articles/114635)). No mean for youth in care or in a mental-health clinic, on these twelve items scored 12–60, was found. The multiple-service sample exists. Its total does not.

Two other numbers that circulate as CYRM-12 means are the wrong scale. Panter-Brick and colleagues’ mean of 50.30 (SD 6.71) belongs to a twelve-item form they built for Syrian refugee and Jordanian host-community adolescents after the standard one-factor CYRM-12 fit poorly; it includes items the survey does not ask ([Panter-Brick et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12868)). A Kolkata school study scored items 1–3, total 12–36, and used its own median of 31 as a cut. A Tehran school mean of 27.8 (SD 4.1) has the same 12–36 range ([Kolkata school PDF](https://www.ijpediatrics.com/index.php/ijcp/article/download/1738/1287); [Tehran psychometric PDF](https://hdq.uswr.ac.ir/article-1-498-en.pdf)). Subtracting 12 does not put a three-point scale onto a five-point scale. There is no standard low-resilience cut for the CYRM-12.

## Trauma and gaming have published rules; four modules do not

The Child and Adolescent Trauma Screen youth self-report is the optional trauma module’s symptom metric. Twenty past-two-week items are scored 0 never, 1 once in a while, 2 half the time, and 3 almost always, and the total runs from **0 to 60** ([APA CATS youth form](https://www.apaservices.org/practice/reimbursement/health-registry/child-adolescent-trauma-screen.pdf)). The published youth event list has **15** yes/no events. The survey dictionary counts 16. Every event type described for the survey appears on the published list; the extra exposure item was not identified, and the score the instrument uses is the symptom total, not the event count. For ages 7–17 the 2017 materials band that total as **below 15** (not clinically elevated), **15–20** (moderate trauma-related distress), and **21 or higher** (probable PTSD) ([CATS scoring tip sheet, 2017](https://uwhatc.uw.edu/PDF/TF-%20CBT/pages/3%20Assessment/Standardized%20Measures/CATS-Scoring-Tip-Sheet-2017.pdf); [ISTSS CATS page](https://istss.org/clinical-resources/child-adolescent-trauma-assessments/child-and-adolescent-trauma-screen/)). The original psychometric paper, in US, German, and Norwegian trauma-exposed samples, supported the four DSM-5 clusters (alpha 0.88–0.94) and said interview validation of the cut was still pending ([Sachser et al., 2017](https://edoc.ku.de/id/eprint/19151/)). The 2022 interview study kept **≥21** as the sensitive screen for probable PTSD and added **≥25** when higher specificity is wanted, against the CAPS-CA-5 in 121 interviewed children and adolescents, area under the curve 0.88 ([Sachser et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9344962/)). In that paper’s self-report clinical sample, N = 283, ages 7–17, mean age 12.21 (SD 3.16), 59% female, from trauma services in the United States, Norway, and Germany, the DSM-5 total averaged **24.16 (SD 12.26)**. Country means were United States **22.70 (SD 12.57, n = 95)**, Norway **22.36 (SD 11.66, n = 100)**, and Germany **27.80 (SD 11.91, n = 88)**. The clinical mean sits between the two thresholds. It includes foster, adoptive, and child-welfare placements. No unselected school or community mean, and no refugee-sample mean, was found for the 0–60 youth self-report. Separate ICD-11 cuts (≥7 sensitive, ≥9 more specific) apply to a shorter item set. They are not cuts on the 0–60 total.

The nine-item social-media and gaming blocks are two ICD-11 scales from the same Hamburg group, not one addiction score. GADIS-A is gaming and SOMEDIS-A is social media. Items 1–9 cover the last 12 months and are scored strongly disagree 0 through strongly agree 4, so each sum runs from **0 to 36**. Each published scale also has a tenth item on how often the problems occurred, and the time criterion is met at “longer periods” or “nearly daily” ([Paschke, Austermann, and Thomasius, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7230491/); [Paschke, Austermann, and Thomasius, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8100192/)). The survey blocks, as specified, are the nine symptom items. Disorder in the papers is not a single 0–36 cut. For GADIS-A, negative consequences are items 3, 6, 7, 8, and 9, and cognitive-behavioural symptoms are items 1, 2, 4, and 5. The rule applied to frequent gamers was factor 1 above 5, factor 2 above 9, total at least 12, and the time criterion. Youden’s point on the total was 12.5. That full rule classified **3.7%** (30 of 819; 95% CI 2.4–5.0) of German adolescents aged 10–17 who gamed at least weekly. Among those not classified as disordered (n = 789), mean factor scores were **2.13 (SE 0.11)** and **4.94 (SE 0.13)**; among the 30 classified as disordered they were **13.30 (SE 0.78)** and **13.53 (SE 0.39)**. In the wider weekly-gamer group (n = 1,014) the mean age was 13.01 (SD 2.36). For SOMEDIS-A the factor cuts differ: the applied rule was factor 1 above 6, factor 2 above 8, and the time criterion, with no single 0–36 threshold. Prevalence among German adolescents aged 10–17 who used social media at least weekly was **3.3%** (31 of 931; 95% CI 2.2–4.5), mean age 13.67 (SD 2.19), during COVID-19 fieldwork. Undisordered youth (n = 900) averaged **2.43 (SE 0.10)** and **4.81 (SE 0.12)** on the two factors; the disordered group averaged **13.13 (SE 0.88)** and **12.45 (SE 0.49)**. A full-sample mean and SD of the 0–36 sum was not printed for either scale, and no treatment-sample mean was reported. The nine-item yes/no Social Media Disorder Scale and Internet Gaming Disorder Scale appear in those papers only as validity criteria. Their 0–9 counts are a different metric.

The remaining four modules do not have a youth normal score that can be attached to the survey.

The 23-item “how easy” block matches the stems of the German mental-health literacy questionnaire MHLS-GER-Q24, an adaptation of the European Health Literacy Survey in which higher scores mean information feels easier to manage. The published form has **24** items. Item 8 is about workplace offers. Dropping that item would produce a 23-item block, and the sources do not confirm that the survey did so. In 2,007 German internet users the mean item score was **2.42** on a scale from 1 “very difficult” to 4 “very easy,” alpha was 0.96, and 54.3% of responses were “very difficult” or “rather difficult” ([European Journal of Public Health abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurpub/ckaf161.1928)). A public report of about 2,000 adults aged 18 and over, plus 500 apprentices, surveyed online in 2024, classified **86%** of the general-population sample as low mental-health literacy; among apprentices, **79.6%** were low and **46.6%** were in the lowest, “inadequate,” level ([Impact, December 2024](https://www.wortundbildverlag.de/sites/wortundbildverlag.de/files/2024-12/AU_Impact_12-2024.pdf)). The numeric index cuts that separate inadequate, problematic, sufficient, and excellent were not in the sources read. No school-age mean was found. Apprentices are the youngest group reported. The O’Connor Mental Health Literacy Scale, including its German knowledge-and-attitude translation, uses different items and a sum that can run from 35 to 160. That sum is not a benchmark for this block.

The section named as the Digital Addiction Scale for Children matches Hawi, Samaha, and Griffiths’ 25-item scale for ages about 9–12, scored 1 never to 5 always, total **25–125** if all items are kept, with higher meaning more digital addiction. The development sample was 822 pupils in grades 4–7, alpha 0.936, and the abstract reports no mean and no cutoff ([Hawi, Samaha, and Griffiths, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1089/cyber.2019.0132)). A Spanish school sample of 977 pupils, mean age **10.65**, and a Turkish sample of 670, mean age **11.34**, likewise printed no total mean and no diagnostic cut in the texts retrieved ([Spanish DASC study](https://doi.org/10.5964/ejop.17937); [Turkish psychometric paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10122085/)). The survey export does not show the items, so retention of all 25 statements and of the 1–5 metric was not checked. Samples stop around age 14 and are mostly under 12. There is no probable-addiction threshold in these sources comparable to the gaming or trauma rules.

The Columbia suicide screener is six yes/no questions, not a symptom total, and it has no normal score. On the school form, everyone is asked about a past-month wish to be dead and past-month thoughts of killing oneself; method, intent, and plan are asked only if thoughts are present; a lifetime behaviour question is asked of everyone, with a past-three-month probe. A yes to the wish, the thoughts, a method, or lifetime behaviour is triaged to a behavioural-health referral. A yes to intent, a plan, or behaviour in the past three months is the higher-acuity result ([C-SSRS school screener](https://cssrs.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/C-SSRS-Screener-with-Triage-Points-for-Schools-2026.pdf)). No large European community prevalence of this recent six-item screener was found. Where a C-SSRS version was applied to all paediatric emergency patients, about one-fifth screened positive (Hill and colleagues, 19%); among psychiatric emergency patients the positive range was **66% to 86%** ([Scudder et al., 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.916731/full)). In 284 US probation-referred youth, past-month endorsement was **24.7%** wish to be dead, **21.5%** suicidal ideation, **7.8%** method, **10.2%** intent, and **3.2%** intent with a plan; behaviour in the past three months was **2.1%** and lifetime behaviour **17.3%** ([legal-system youth sample](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12530505/)). Those are emergency and justice samples. Studies also disagree on what “positive” means. The school triage above is the Columbia form’s rule.

The sleep items traced to the Norwegian Mother, Father and Child Cohort are questionnaire questions, not a named scale. The 18-year documentation lists difficulty falling asleep, night waking, daytime sleepiness, duration of the problem, and weekday sleep length, adapted from the Karolinska Sleep Questionnaire, the Bergen Insomnia Scale, and a sleep diary, and it defines no summed MoBa sleep score ([MoBa 18-year instrument documentation](https://www.fhi.no/contentassets/1016188d845f4c5f8fa57266c454ad8c/instrument-documentation-18-year_.pdf)). The youth form’s sleep block is a set of branching follow-ups with the same character ([MoBaUng questionnaire 6](https://www.fhi.no/globalassets/dokumenterfiler/studier/den-norske-mor-far-og-barn--undersokelsenmoba/sporreskjemaer/mobaung-skjema-6---versjon-c---20200910.pdf)). The Bergen Insomnia Scale is six items. It does not match a block of about twenty questions, and the cohort documentation describes adaptation rather than administration of that scale. No MoBa sleep total, cutoff, or adolescent norm was found. Without the item text, these answers cannot be scored as the Insomnia Severity Index either.

## Conclusion

The survey’s own arithmetic is what separates a usable benchmark from a neighbouring one. Attention can take the 2009 youth flag because the six items and the 0–2 anchors match, and it still cannot take a community mean, because the printed means are parent report at the wrong age. KIDSCREEN-10 can take a European adolescent percentile because the manual publishes a linear bridge from the raw sum, and it still cannot take “T below 40,” because that rule sits on a Rasch score the file does not contain. The CYRM-12 can take one school mean and cannot take a cut the centre has refused to set. The ACE-IQ can be read as two WHO totals, and the youth distribution an analyst would want for Europe was not published, so a Malawian frequency mean of 2.9 or a Slovak adult binary mean of 2.7 answers a different question. Trauma symptoms and the two German media scales are the optional modules with an external rule, and each rule carries a sample — trauma clinics, or German weekly users — plus, for the media scales, a time item the nine-item blocks do not include.

What follows for scoring is a split file. Columns with a matched external anchor can carry that anchor’s sample, age, and informant in the same sentence as the number. Columns whose literature is a T-score, a one-item-different sum, an adult ACE table, or a scale the export does not actually contain are better reported as the distribution the survey itself produces. Filling those cells with T = 50, with “four or more” from the original ACE study, or with a resilience threshold from the 17-item revision would rank youth against a score they were never given.
